# Time-to-event and dynamic risk prediction for post-operative orthopedic RTM: discrete-time (pooled logistic) hazard models, landmarking, competing risks, and honest evaluation at 10–1000 patients with rare labels

## Summary

The correct frame for "will this patient have a complication in the next N days" is a **discrete-time hazard model fit by pooled logistic regression on person-day rows**, not a continuous-time Cox model. It handles time-varying wearable covariates natively, produces a daily-updating conditional hazard h(d) that composes into any horizon risk via 1 − ∏(1−h), is fit by ordinary (penalized) logistic regression, and Suresh et al. (BMC Med Res Methodol 2022) show discrete-time models can match or beat continuous-time Cox on predictive accuracy. Cox with time-varying covariates is structurally wrong for your product: lifelines' own docs state CoxTimeVaryingFitter cannot predict survival functions for new subjects because future covariate paths are unknown — it is an inference tool, not a rolling-score tool. Joint models (JM/JMbayes2) are not feasible at your scale: a recent simulation review found that with fewer than ~70 events convergence was low and bias persisted even in converged fits, and there is no maintained Python implementation. Deep survival (DeepSurv/DeepHit/pycox) is inappropriate — pycox's own benchmarks run n=1,904 to 2.8M, and even at n=9,105 (SUPPORT) nnet-survival scored C=0.732 versus 0.734 for plain Cox PH, i.e. no gain at 10× your best-case sample. The honest arithmetic is decisive: at 1,000 patients with a 3% complication rate you have ~30 events, which at 10 events-per-parameter buys you about **three** free parameters — so the right first ship is your existing expert-weighted composite exposed as a calibrated daily hazard, validated on operating characteristics rather than fit statistics. There is a direct precedent for this: Tilquin et al. (PLOS Digit Health 2026) achieved AUC 0.88, 74% sensitivity at 85% specificity, and 4.4 ± 3.1 days of lead time with an **unsupervised** vital-sign deviation score on 220 remotely monitored patients with only 42 events — almost exactly your architecture and your data volume. The two things that will actually break you are target leakage from patients who stop wearing the device when they feel ill (informative censoring) and class-imbalance "corrections", which are documented to destroy calibration.

## Findings

### Discrete-time survival on a person-period dataset is the validated frame, and can beat continuous-time Cox
*[strong]*

Suresh, Severn & Ghosh (2022) formalize it: split follow-up into J contiguous intervals (t₀,t₁],…,(t_{J−1},t_J]; each subject contributes one row per interval in which they are still at risk, carrying a binary failure indicator, covariates, and an interval identifier. The discrete hazard is λ_ij = Pr(T_i ∈ A_j | T_i > t_{j−1}, X_i) and survival is S_i(t|X) = ∏_{j: t_j ≤ t} (1 − λ_ij). The likelihood factorizes into independent binomial terms, so ANY binary classifier fit to the stacked rows is a valid estimator of the conditional hazard. They compared discrete-time logistic, penalized/LASSO, random forest, gradient boosting and neural nets against continuous-time Cox PH and random survival forests, and concluded some discrete-time models achieved BETTER prediction performance than Cox PH, with 'limited loss in predictive accuracy' from discretization. Caveat: the paper's exposition assumes time-fixed covariates and explicitly does not develop time-varying covariate methodology — you are extending it (legitimately, since the factorized likelihood permits row-level covariates).

> Suresh K, Severn C, Ghosh D. Survival prediction models: an introduction to discrete-time modeling. BMC Med Res Methodol 2022;22:207. PMID 35883032, PMCID PMC9316420, DOI 10.1186/s12874-022-01679-6

### Number of time intervals barely matters — discretization is robust
*[strong]*

Gensheimer & Narasimhan tested four discretization schemes (uniform 1-year, uniform 1-month, uniform 1-week, and exponentially widening intervals) on the same data and got an IDENTICAL C-index of 0.66 across all four. They used 15–40 intervals in practice, widened with increasing follow-up so each interval holds roughly equal event counts, and cite prior guidance that at least ten intervals should be used to avoid bias. Implication for you: daily intervals over a 90-day post-op window (90 rows/patient) is far more than sufficient resolution, and you should NOT worry that daily granularity is 'too fine' — but you SHOULD widen the effective resolution of the baseline hazard by using a smooth function of post-op day rather than 90 dummies.

> Gensheimer MF, Narasimhan B. A scalable discrete-time survival model for neural networks. PeerJ 2019;7:e6257. PMID 30701130, PMCID PMC6348952, DOI 10.7717/peerj.6257

### Baseline hazard: use a restricted cubic spline in post-op day, not J dummies
*[moderate]*

The person-day model needs an explicit, flexible baseline hazard term — this is the one thing that replaces Cox's nonparametric baseline. Options are (a) one dummy per interval (fully nonparametric, exactly Cox-like flexibility, but costs J parameters — catastrophic at 30 events), (b) a low-order polynomial in time (used e.g. by Bere et al. 2025 in a geoadditive discrete-time model), (c) restricted cubic splines. At your event count, use an RCS with 3–4 knots (2–3 df) placed at post-op days ~5, 14, 30 (or at quantiles of observed event days). Rationale: post-op complication hazard is strongly non-monotone (surgical-site infection peaks ~days 5–21, VTE peaks earlier, stiffness/MUA much later), so a linear or log-linear term in day will misfit badly, while 90 dummies will not fit at all. Suresh et al. do not benchmark basis choice — this recommendation is standard practice rather than a head-to-head result.

> Suresh et al. 2022 (person-period framing); Bere et al., BMC Womens Health 2025, PMID 40128696 (polynomial baseline hazard in discrete-time model)

### Link function: cloglog gives you Cox hazard ratios, logit gives odds ratios — at daily hazards they coincide
*[moderate]*

A complementary log-log link on the person-period rows yields the grouped-proportional-hazards model, whose coefficients are interpretable as Cox log-hazard-ratios (the discrete-time model is the exact grouped-data analogue of Cox PH). A logit link yields discrete-time odds ratios. Because your per-day hazard will be tiny (a 3% 30-day risk implies a daily hazard on the order of 0.001), OR ≈ HR to within a fraction of a percent, so the choice is immaterial numerically. Recommendation: use logit, because it gives you the entire sklearn/statsmodels ecosystem (penalization, calibration, probability outputs) for free; use cloglog only if a clinical reviewer wants coefficients labelled as hazard ratios. statsmodels supports it directly: sm.GLM(y, X, family=sm.families.Binomial(link=sm.families.links.CLogLog())).

> Standard grouped-proportional-hazards result (Prentice & Gloeckler); framing consistent with Suresh et al. 2022, PMID 35883032

### Repeated measures: point estimates are valid, standard errors are not — cluster by patient, and split CV by patient
*[strong]*

The discrete-time likelihood is a genuine product of conditional Bernoullis, so stacking ~90 rows per patient and running logistic regression is NOT a naive-independence hack — the point estimates are proper maximum likelihood. What IS wrong is the standard errors and any row-level cross-validation, because unmodeled between-patient heterogeneity (frailty) induces within-patient correlation. Fix with cluster-robust sandwich SEs: statsmodels GLM/Logit `.fit(cov_type='cluster', cov_kwds={'groups': df.patient_id})`, or statsmodels GEE (`smf.gee(formula, 'patient_id', data, family=sm.families.Binomial(), cov_struct=sm.cov_struct.Independence())`) which reports robust sandwich SEs by construction. Independence working correlation + robust SEs is the right choice here — an Exchangeable structure would fight the time-varying baseline hazard. Critically: every train/test split and every CV fold must be at the PATIENT level (sklearn StratifiedGroupKFold with groups=patient_id). Row-level splitting leaks the same patient into train and test and will inflate AUC by a large, unquantifiable margin.

> statsmodels GEE documentation, https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/gee.html

### Cox with time-varying covariates cannot produce a rolling risk score — lifelines says so explicitly
*[strong]*

lifelines CoxTimeVaryingFitter takes long format (id, start exclusive, stop inclusive, event, covariates) and fits via `ctv.fit(df, id_col='id', event_col='event', start_col='start', stop_col='stop')` with a `penalizer` argument. But its documentation states that 'prediction in a time-varying setting is not trivial' and that the model CANNOT predict survival functions for new subjects, because doing so would require knowing their future covariate trajectories. It only exposes hazard at observed points plus baseline cumulative hazard/survival. It is therefore an ETIOLOGIC tool (does rising RHR associate with complication?), not a product surface. The docs also warn about reverse causation — an impending event drives the covariate — and offer a `delay` parameter in `add_covariate_to_timeline()` as mitigation. That reverse-causation warning applies directly to you: a patient whose infection is already brewing has an elevated RHR *because* of it.

> lifelines documentation, Time varying survival regression, https://lifelines.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Time%20varying%20survival%20regression.html

### Joint models (JM/JMbayes2) are not feasible below ~70 events — this is a hard stop, not a preference
*[strong]*

A simulation study of R joint-model implementations found that with fewer than 70 events convergence was LOW and bias PERSISTED EVEN IN MODELS THAT CONVERGED; with observation-to-event ratios below 2, convergence declined further. The `JM` package showed considerable bias and frequent convergence failures; `joineRML` was approximately unbiased and robust; `JMbayes2` was largely unbiased but degraded under two conditions. Frequentist packages systematically underestimated baseline covariate effects. The same study found time-varying Cox and two-stage approaches achieved more robust convergence than several joint-model implementations. Separately, joint models with many longitudinal markers (you have 6+) face a documented computational blow-up from the high number of random effects. There is no maintained Python joint-model package — you would be shelling out to R. Verdict: do not build joint models at 10–1000 patients with single-digit-percent events. Revisit only past ~200–300 adjudicated events.

> Heege et al. 2026 simulation comparison of R joint-model implementations (via Europe PMC); Hashemi et al., Stat Med 2025, PMID 40462390 (random-effects blow-up with many markers)

### Landmarking is the robust middle path, but its ML variants need n≥1000
*[moderate]*

A head-to-head simulation plus kidney-transplant validation found joint models superior when marker–outcome relationships are LINEAR (higher AUC, lower Brier), but a landmarking gradient boosting model (LGBM) outperformed both classical methods under complex/nonlinear relationships — and specifically at larger samples (n=1000–1500, NOT at n=300–650), higher censoring (90% vs 30–50%), and later landmark times. A separate three-dataset comparison found landmarking, joint models, and a delayed-kernel approach 'comparable' in predictive accuracy with no clear winner. Read this as: landmarking with a SIMPLE model (penalized logistic) is safe at your scale; landmarking with boosted trees needs ~1000+ patients before it earns its complexity.

> Shabani et al., BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 2025, PMID 41068878; Davies et al., Stat Methods Med Res 2024, PMID 39211944

### Deep survival models do not beat Cox even at n≈9,000 — they are categorically wrong for you
*[strong]*

On the SUPPORT dataset (9,105 patients; 6,373 train / 2,732 test; 39 predictors), Gensheimer's nnet-survival scored C-index 0.732, Cox-nnet 0.735, DeepSurv 0.730, and plain Cox PH 0.734 — a spread of 0.005, i.e. nothing. Brier scores at 6mo/1yr/3yr were within 0.003 of each other. pycox (the standard Python home of DeepSurv, CoxTime, DeepHit, PMF, MTLR, PC-Hazard, Logistic-Hazard, BCESurv) ships benchmark datasets of n=1,904 (METABRIC) to 2.8M (KKBox), and its README describes the package as 'still in its early stages of development'. It requires torch and torchtuples — a heavy dependency for a Lambda deployment. DeepHit's one genuine advantage is native competing risks, which you can get far more cheaply from a multinomial person-day model. Verdict: do not use pycox. If you ever want the neural discrete-time formulation, note it is literally the same likelihood as your pooled logistic model with a bigger function class.

> Gensheimer & Narasimhan, PeerJ 2019, PMID 30701130; pycox repository, https://github.com/havakv/pycox

### Random survival forests: usable only in the landmark frame, and sksurv's defaults are already small-data-tuned
*[strong]*

sksurv.ensemble.RandomSurvivalForest defaults: n_estimators=100, max_depth=None, min_samples_split=6, min_samples_leaf=3, max_features='sqrt', bootstrap=True, oob_score=False, low_memory=False; splits are chosen by the log-rank statistic; predict() returns a risk score, predict_survival_function() returns Kaplan-Meier step functions per leaf, predict_cumulative_hazard_function() returns Nelson-Aalen. Feature importance is NOT exposed as an attribute — use sklearn.inspection.permutation_importance. Setting low_memory=True disables survival/hazard function prediction. Crucially, the documentation does not support time-varying covariates at all, so an RSF can only consume ONE row per patient — meaning your only way to use it dynamically is a landmark dataset (one row per patient-landmark, features summarizing the trailing window). At 30 events, an RSF will overfit; raise min_samples_leaf to ~15–20 and max_depth to 3–4 if you try it at all.

> scikit-survival API docs, https://scikit-survival.readthedocs.io/en/stable/api/generated/sksurv.ensemble.RandomSurvivalForest.html

### Competing risks: cause-specific for 'why', Fine-Gray for 'what is this patient's absolute risk' — and Kaplan-Meier lies
*[strong]*

Austin, Lee & Fine state it directly: the cause-specific hazard is 'the instantaneous rate of occurrence of the kth event in subjects who are currently event free', and is 'better suited for studying the etiology of diseases'; the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard keeps subjects who experienced a COMPETING event in the risk set, directly models the cumulative incidence function CIF_k(t) = Pr(T ≤ t, D = k), and is 'preferable when the focus is on estimating actual risks and prognosis'. Their worked example: 5-year cardiovascular death was 43.0% by Kaplan-Meier versus 36.8% by CIF — a 6.2 percentage-point overestimate from ignoring competing risks. For MedPull: within a 30–90 day post-op orthopedic window, competing events (death, unrelated reoperation) are genuinely rare, so cause-specific modeling is adequate and Fine-Gray's extra machinery is not yet earned. The elegant part: in the DISCRETE-TIME frame you get CIFs for free by fitting a multinomial logit on person-day rows (outcome ∈ {none, infection, VTE, readmission, competing}) and accumulating cause-specific daily hazards through the survival product — no Fine-Gray implementation needed, which matters because neither lifelines nor scikit-survival ships a Fine-Gray model in Python.

> Austin PC, Lee DS, Fine JP. Introduction to the Analysis of Survival Data in the Presence of Competing Risks. Circulation 2016;133:601-609. PMCID PMC4741409

### Closest published analogue to your rolling score: 215 patients, step counts, AUC 0.82, and longer horizons predicted better
*[strong]*

Bressman et al. studied exactly your question — dynamic post-discharge risk from remote-monitoring step counts. n=215 participants aged 55+, outcome readmission-or-death over 30–180 days. LightGBM reached mean AUC 0.82 versus 0.76 for logistic regression across all configurations, and was well calibrated (Hosmer–Lemeshow p=0.96) where logistic regression was miscalibrated. The two findings that should shape your product: performance IMPROVED with longer prospective horizons, and was INSENSITIVE to retrospective window length (they tested 3–10 day prospective windows). Top features were recent steps, distance traveled, length of stay, vitals and BMI. Practical translation: prefer a 14- or 30-day horizon over a 7-day one for the headline score, and do not over-engineer the lookback window — a 7-day trailing summary is as good as a longer one.

> Bressman E, Park SH, Greysen SR, Chen J. Optimizing temporal windows for wearable-augmented post-discharge risk prediction: a methods study. JAMIA 2026;33(7):1314-1321. PMID 42035291, DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocag057

### An UNSUPERVISED deviation score reached AUC 0.88 with 220 patients and 42 events — this is the strongest argument for shipping your current engine
*[strong]*

Tilquin et al. built the Bora Vital Sign Standard Score (BVS3), an unsupervised statistical score over home-collected SpO₂, breathing rate and heart rate from a CE-certified wristband. 220 COPD patients monitored 6 months; 42 validated exacerbations in 39 patients; median adherence 86%. Reported: AUC 0.88 (95% CI 0.83–0.92), sensitivity 74% at specificity 85%, overall accuracy 84.8%, and anticipation of exacerbations 4.4 ± 3.1 days before clinical confirmation. This is an almost exact structural match to MedPull's composite deviation index (same metric family, same wrist device class, same 'deviation from personal baseline' logic, comparable cohort size), and it required NO outcome labels to construct — only labels to evaluate. It is the empirical warrant for shipping the expert-weighted score first and treating the learned hazard model as a later upgrade.

> Tilquin F, et al. Short-term prediction of COPD exacerbations based on wearable vital sign monitoring. PLOS Digit Health 2026;5(5):e0001405. PMID 42207714, PMCID PMC13218495

### Informative censoring from device disengagement is an acknowledged blind spot in the entire wearable literature
*[moderate]*

A targeted search of wearable/digital-health studies for informative dropout, MNAR mechanisms, or IPCW returned essentially nothing: studies report adherence figures (e.g. 5.8 days/week smartwatch wear, 95.5% wearing ≥8h per valid day; another with >90% data completion and 71% full compliance) and then apply multiple imputation or ITT without examining whether disengaged users differ systematically in health trajectory or whether non-wear correlates with deterioration. The methodological fix is IPCW: fit a daily 'still wearing / still engaged' logistic model on observed covariates, weight each person-day by the inverse of the cumulative probability of remaining uncensored. Recent work shows STABILIZED weights generally improve efficiency versus unstabilized, but can INCREASE bias when the outcome model is misspecified — and IPCW's validity rests on an untestable no-unmeasured-confounding-of-censoring assumption, so a sensitivity analysis is mandatory. The specific leak for MedPull: a patient who feels ill and stops wearing the device is censored precisely BECAUSE of their unobserved risk. Train naively and you will learn 'no data = no risk', which is the exact inversion of the truth.

> Xuan et al., Stat Methods Med Res 2025;34(2):286-306, PMID 39668583 (IPCW vs per-protocol); Xuan et al., Stat Methods Med Res 2026;35(1):40-60, PMID 41172039 (stabilization); Europe PMC survey of wearable-adherence studies showing no MNAR analysis

### Do NOT apply class-imbalance corrections — they are documented to damage calibration
*[strong]*

A simulation study with van Calster and van Smeden among the authors found that class imbalance corrections (oversampling, undersampling, SMOTE) HARM calibration of ML-based clinical prediction models, and that models developed WITHOUT any correction had equal or better calibration in out-of-sample evaluation. Since your outcome will be a 1–5% daily-to-monthly event and your person-day dataset will be ~0.03–0.1% positive rows, the temptation to rebalance will be strong and must be resisted. Rare positives are a precision problem, not a training-set-composition problem: keep the natural prevalence, use penalized likelihood, evaluate with PR-AUC and calibration rather than accuracy, and choose the operating threshold from a decision-analytic argument (alerts per clinician per week) rather than from 0.5.

> Carriero A, Luijken K, de Hond A, et al. The Harms of Class Imbalance Corrections for Machine Learning Based Prediction Models. Stat Med 2025. PMID 39865585, DOI 10.1002/sim.10320

### Evaluation stack: Uno's IPCW C-index, cumulative/dynamic AUC and integrated Brier — with a mandatory tau truncation
*[strong]*

scikit-survival provides the four metrics you need. concordance_index_censored() is Harrell's estimator and exhibits documented UPWARD bias at censoring ≥49% — with post-op monitoring you will be at 95%+ censoring, so this metric is unusable for you. Use concordance_index_ipcw() (Uno) with an explicit `tau`. cumulative_dynamic_auc(survival_train, survival_test, estimate, times) gives the time-dependent AUC; brier_score() and integrated_brier_score() give calibration+discrimination jointly and require a model exposing predict_survival_function(). All IPCW-based metrics depend on correctly estimating the CENSORING distribution, and the docs warn the final evaluation time point must have non-zero censoring probability after it — set tau conservatively at roughly the 80th percentile of observed times when censoring is heavy (>70%). For hyperparameter search, sksurv ships as_concordance_index_ipcw_scorer(), as_cumulative_dynamic_auc_scorer() and as_integrated_brier_score_scorer() for GridSearchCV.

> scikit-survival, Evaluating Survival Models, https://scikit-survival.readthedocs.io/en/stable/user_guide/evaluating-survival-models.html

### Calibration, not discrimination, is what makes a risk score clinically usable — and it needs its own sample size
*[strong]*

Van Calster et al. call calibration 'the Achilles heel of predictive analytics' and note that reliable calibration CURVES require substantially larger samples than AUC estimates. The reporting targets are calibration intercept (target 0) and calibration slope (target 1), plus a flexible calibration curve estimated by loess or restricted cubic splines rather than decile bins. A related paper argues there is 'no such thing as a validated prediction model' — performance heterogeneity across sites, devices and time is inevitable, so you should plan continuous performance monitoring and periodic recalibration rather than a one-time validation. For your multi-site future, clustered flexible calibration plots (mixed-model or two-stage meta-analytic) are the right tool. Report against TRIPOD+AI and self-assess with PROBAST+AI before any clinical claim.

> Van Calster B, McLernon DJ, van Smeden M, Wynants L, Steyerberg EW. Calibration: the Achilles heel of predictive analytics. BMC Med 2019;17:230. PMID 31842878; Van Calster et al., BMC Med 2023, PMID 36829188; Collins et al., TRIPOD+AI, BMJ 2024, PMID 38626948

### Firth penalized logistic is the textbook rare-event fix but the Python package will NOT install on your backend
*[strong]*

Firth's penalized-likelihood logistic regression (Firth 1993; Heinze & Schemper, Stat Med 2002;21:2409-2419) removes the small-sample bias and separation problems that plague logistic regression with rare events — exactly your regime. The Python implementation is `firthlogist` (v0.5.0, released 2022-08-07, MIT, sklearn-compatible API, params max_iter=25, max_halfstep=25, max_stepsize=5, fit_intercept=True, alpha=0.05, plus skip_pvals/skip_ci and profile-likelihood variants). BUT its metadata declares requires-python >=3.8,<3.11, while recovery-copilot/backend/pyproject.toml sets requires-python = '>=3.12'. It will not resolve, and it has not been released in ~4 years. Practical substitute: L2-penalized logistic via sklearn LogisticRegression(penalty='l2', C=…, solver='lbfgs'), which delivers the same shrinkage benefit and is what Riley's shrinkage-based sample-size framework assumes anyway. Reserve Firth for offline analysis in R (`logistf`) if you hit true separation.

> firthlogist PyPI metadata, https://pypi.org/pypi/firthlogist/json; project config at /Users/steve/Documents/GitHub/MedPullKioskKanthi/recovery-copilot/backend/pyproject.toml


## Implications for backend

- Add a materialized person-day feature table now, before any modeling. Schema: (patient_id, postop_day, calendar_date, feature_vector_json, composite_index, coverage_7d, days_since_last_sync, at_risk BOOL, outcome_code, model_version). The engine in /Users/steve/Documents/GitHub/MedPullKioskKanthi/recovery-copilot/backend/app/engine/ already computes everything needed (baseline.py, deviation.py, composite.py, confidence.py, pipeline.py) but computes it transiently for display. The single highest-value engineering task in this whole domain is PERSISTING what the engine already computes, with an as-of timestamp, so that a hazard model can be fit honestly later. Enforce the leak-safe rule at write time: a row for day d may only contain data whose source timestamp is <= end of day d. If you retroactively backfill a row when late-arriving wearable data syncs, you must either version the row or exclude backfilled rows from training.
- Add an outcomes/events table you do not currently appear to have: (patient_id, event_date, event_type ENUM[infection, vte, readmission, mua, revision, death, other], adjudicated_by, adjudication_date, source). Plus a per-patient last_known_contact_date. Without an explicit censoring date, no survival model of any kind is estimable — 'no event recorded' is ambiguous between 'stayed healthy' and 'we lost them'. This is the binding constraint on everything else in this report; ~30 adjudicated events is the entry price for the learned model and you cannot count them if you do not record them.
- Change the role of confidence.py from a display gate to a modeling input. coverage(), MIN_METRICS_PER_DAY=3, GATE=0.4 currently produce a ConfidenceLevel used to suppress output. Two changes: (a) emit coverage_score, days_with_data and days_since_last_sync as first-class FEATURES into the person-day table, because a coverage collapse is itself a risk signal (the patient who feels ill stops wearing the watch); (b) make the gate asymmetric — LOW confidence must suppress the reassuring statement while still permitting a 'we have lost visibility on this patient' alert. As written, a disengaging patient produces silence, which the provider will read as 'fine'. That is the single most dangerous behaviour in the current design and it is a pure logic fix requiring no data.
- Keep Lambda inference dependency-free. The recommended discrete-time model serves as h = sigmoid(spline_basis(day) @ beta_time + x @ beta) — a numpy dot product against ~10 frozen floats. Fit offline with statsmodels/scikit-learn in a dev dependency group; ship only the coefficient JSON (with model_version, fit_date, training n, event count, and the calibration intercept/slope) into the deployment artifact. Do NOT add scikit-learn, scikit-survival, lightgbm, torch or pycox to the runtime dependencies in pyproject.toml — the current runtime list (fastapi, sqlalchemy, pydantic, numpy, scipy, pandas, httpx) is already near the practical Lambda package ceiling, and none of these are needed at inference time.
- pyproject.toml sets requires-python = '>=3.12', which blocks firthlogist (requires-python >=3.8,<3.11, last released 2022). Do not plan around Firth logistic in Python. Use sklearn LogisticRegression(penalty='l2') for the shrinkage, which is what the sample-size literature assumes anyway; if you hit true separation on a rare cause, do that one analysis offline in R with logistf rather than downgrading the backend Python.
- Version and store every score you emit alongside its exact input feature vector and model_version. This turns your production traffic into a prospective validation cohort at zero incremental cost, and it is the only way to satisfy Van Calster's point that models are never validated once but must be monitored and recalibrated over time. Practically: a predictions table (patient_id, postop_day, model_version, risk_7d, risk_14d, risk_30d, composite_index, confidence_level, feature_vector_json, emitted_at). Without this you will be unable to answer 'was the score right?' six months from now.
- Reframe the API contract around a HORIZON. The current risk.py emits an ordered tier. The tier should carry an explicit horizon and an explicit confidence, e.g. {tier: 'elevated', horizon_days: 14, basis: 'expert_weighted_v1', calibrated: false, confidence: 'medium'}. Shipping `calibrated: false` is not a weakness — it is what lets you later ship `calibrated: true` with a probability without breaking clients or overclaiming today. The LLM narrative layer must be forbidden from converting a tier into a percentage; that boundary already exists in your architecture (LLM writes narrative, never numbers) and must be enforced for horizons too.
- When outcome data arrives, the composite index should enter the hazard model as a SINGLE scalar covariate rather than being replaced by its six components. At 30 events you can afford roughly three parameters; the expert-weighted composite compresses six metrics into one degree of freedom, which is exactly the right trade. Only unpack it into components once you exceed ~100 events, and then only if patient-grouped CV shows a real PR-AUC gain over the composite-as-scalar version.

  - needs: ZERO labels to ship. ~30–50 adjudicated events (≈1,000 patients at 3–5% event rate) before attaching a calibrated probability. ~100 events before claiming a validated AUC with a usable confidence interval.
  - why: Tilquin et al. achieved AUC 0.88 / 74% sensitivity at 85% specificity / 4.4±3.1 days lead time with an unsupervised deviation score on 220 patients and 42 events — no labels required to build it. You cannot beat this with a learned model at 30 events, and attempting to will produce an overfit score that looks better in CV and worse in clinic.
- **PRIMARY LEARNED MODEL — rolling 'risk of complication in next 7/14/30 days' that updates every day as wearable data arrives** — Discrete-time survival via pooled logistic regression on person-day rows. Build one row per (patient, post-op day) for every day the patient is at risk and under observation. Outcome y=1 on the day the adjudicated complication occurs, 0 otherwise; rows stop at event, at last contact (censor), or at day 90. Fit logit h(d | x) = s(d)·β_time + x_d·β. Horizon risk for a patient standing at day t: R_H(t) = 1 − ∏_{d=t+1}^{t+H} (1 − ĥ(d | x_t)), holding covariates at their last observed value (a last-value-carried-forward approximation — state this explicitly in documentation, it is the standard landmark assumption and is what makes this tractable without a joint model). via `statsmodels>=0.14 (`sm.GLM(y, X, family=sm.families.Binomial()).fit(cov_type='cluster', cov_kwds={'groups': patient_id})`) for coefficients + honest SEs offline; patsy/formulaic for the spline basis (`cr(postop_day, df=4)`). scikit-learn>=1.5 LogisticRegression(penalty='l2') for the penalized production fit. Serve inference in the Lambda as a pure numpy dot product of frozen coefficients — do NOT ship statsmodels or sklearn into the deployment artifact.`
  - params: Baseline hazard: restricted cubic spline in post-op day, 4 knots at days 5/14/30/60 (3 df). Covariates: cap at 3 for the first model, 5–6 once you exceed ~100 events. Start with (a) the composite deviation index as a SINGLE scalar covariate — this is the key trick, it buys you all six metrics for one degree of freedom; (b) days-since-surgery interaction only if events allow; (c) a non-wear/coverage covariate. Penalty: L2, C chosen by StratifiedGroupKFold(n_splits=5, groups=patient_id) maximizing average precision, searched over C ∈ {0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3}. Link: logit (cloglog if a reviewer wants hazard ratios; numerically identical at these hazards). NO class weighting, NO SMOTE. Horizons H ∈ {7, 14, 30} all derived from the SAME fitted daily hazard — one model, three headline numbers.
  - needs: Hard floor: ~30 events for a 3-parameter model at 10 events-per-parameter (i.e. ~1,000 patients at 3%). Comfortable: ~100 events for 5–6 parameters with penalization. Below ~30 events, DO NOT FIT THIS — ship the expert-weighted score instead. Person-day row count is NOT the relevant sample size; the number of independent patients with events is.
  - why: Suresh et al. show discrete-time models match or beat continuous-time Cox for prediction; the person-day format ingests time-varying wearable covariates natively, which Cox-for-prediction cannot do (lifelines explicitly refuses to predict for new subjects with unknown future covariate paths); and the daily hazard composes analytically into any horizon, so you fit once and serve 7/14/30-day answers from the same coefficients. Gensheimer showed the discretization choice is nearly irrelevant (identical C-index across four schemes), so daily intervals cost nothing.
- **COMPETING RISKS — distinguishing infection vs VTE vs readmission-for-pain vs death, and getting absolute risks that sum correctly** — Multinomial (softmax) discrete-time hazard on the same person-day rows: outcome ∈ {no event, infection, VTE, readmission-other, competing/death}. Cause-specific daily hazards h_k(d) come straight out of the softmax; the cumulative incidence function for cause k over horizon H is CIF_k = Σ_{d=t+1}^{t+H} h_k(d) · ∏_{j<d}(1 − Σ_m h_m(j)). This gives properly-behaved absolute risks that sum to the all-cause risk, with no Fine-Gray implementation required — which matters because neither lifelines nor scikit-survival ships a Fine-Gray model in Python. via `scikit-learn LogisticRegression(multi_class='multinomial', solver='lbfgs', penalty='l2') or statsmodels MNLogit. No survival-specific package needed — that is the whole point of the discrete-time frame.`
  - params: Same 4-knot spline baseline per cause (or a shared baseline plus per-cause intercepts if events are very sparse — strongly preferred below 50 events per cause). L2 with C tuned by patient-grouped CV. Collapse any cause with <10 events into an 'other complication' bucket rather than modeling it separately.
  - needs: ≥10 events per modeled cause, absolute floor. Realistically 200+ patients per cause-of-interest. Until then use a single pooled complication outcome — splitting rare events across causes is the fastest way to make an already-underpowered model useless.
  - why: Austin, Lee & Fine: cause-specific hazards for etiology, Fine-Gray for absolute risk/prognosis — and Kaplan-Meier overstates risk when competing events are ignored (43.0% vs 36.8% in their worked example). The discrete-time multinomial gives you the CIF directly, so you get the Fine-Gray-quality ANSWER from a cause-specific-style FIT, in pure sklearn. Deploy this only when it changes clinical action; a single composite 'complication' outcome is the right v1 because it maximizes your event count.
- **HANDLING DISENGAGEMENT — the patient who feels ill and stops wearing the device (informative censoring / target leakage)** — Three layers, in increasing order of sophistication. (1) TREAT NON-WEAR AS A FEATURE, NOT AS MISSINGNESS: add `days_since_last_sync`, `coverage_7d` (you already compute it in confidence.py), and `coverage_slope` as explicit covariates in the hazard model. A sudden coverage drop is clinically informative and must be allowed to RAISE risk, not silently zero it. (2) NEVER LET A LOW-DATA DAY PRODUCE A LOW-RISK OUTPUT: the confidence gate must suppress the reassuring statement ('this patient is fine') while still permitting an alert ('we have lost visibility on this patient'). (3) IPCW for offline evaluation: fit a daily 'remains under observation' logistic model on the same covariates, compute stabilized cumulative weights w_i(d) = P̂(uncensored to d) / P̂(uncensored to d | x), truncate at the 1st/99th percentiles (or cap at 10), and weight the validation metrics. via `scikit-learn for the censoring model; scikit-survival >=0.23 for IPCW-weighted metrics (concordance_index_ipcw, cumulative_dynamic_auc, integrated_brier_score) — these already apply a censoring-distribution correction internally via Ĝ(t). numpy for manual stabilized weights.`
  - params: Censoring model: same L2 logistic on {coverage_7d, days_since_surgery, age, procedure, prior adherence}. Weight truncation at the 99th percentile, or hard cap 10.0. Set tau for all IPCW metrics at the 80th percentile of observed follow-up times (sksurv docs explicitly require non-zero censoring probability beyond tau, and you will be at >90% censoring). Sensitivity analysis: refit under (a) MAR, (b) MNAR-pessimistic where every dropout is assumed to have had the event within 14 days, (c) MNAR-optimistic where no dropout had an event — and REPORT THE RANGE.
  - needs: None extra to implement layers (1) and (2) — do them now, they are pure engineering. IPCW needs the same ~30+ events as the primary model plus an accurate `last_known_contact` date per patient, which you must start capturing immediately in the schema.
  - why: Wearable studies routinely report adherence (86% median in the COPD study, 5.8 days/week in others) and then apply multiple imputation without ever testing whether non-wear correlates with deterioration — a documented blind spot across the literature. For MedPull the mechanism is not hypothetical: a post-op patient with a developing infection is exactly the patient who stops charging their watch. IPCW with stabilized weights is the standard correction, but it rests on an untestable assumption, so the sensitivity range is the honest deliverable, not the point estimate.
- **EVALUATION PROTOCOL — proving the score works without fooling yourselves** — Patient-level nested cross-validation with a strictly prospective flavour: StratifiedGroupKFold(n_splits=5, groups=patient_id) for model selection, plus a temporal holdout (train on patients enrolled before date D, test on those after) as the primary honest estimate. Report, in this order: (1) flexible calibration curve (loess or RCS, not deciles) with calibration slope and intercept; (2) PR-AUC / average precision — NOT ROC-AUC alone, which is optimistic at 0.1% positive rows; (3) time-dependent cumulative/dynamic AUC at days 7/14/30; (4) integrated Brier score and Index of Prediction Accuracy vs a null model; (5) the two numbers clinicians actually care about — alerts per patient per week at the chosen threshold, and median lead time before clinical detection. via `scikit-survival>=0.23 (cumulative_dynamic_auc, integrated_brier_score, concordance_index_ipcw, and the as_*_scorer GridSearchCV wrappers); scikit-learn (average_precision_score, calibration_curve, StratifiedGroupKFold, permutation_importance); statsmodels for cluster-robust inference. Offline/dev dependency group only.`
  - params: tau = 80th percentile of observed follow-up. Evaluation time grid = days 7, 14, 21, 30. Use concordance_index_ipcw (Uno), NEVER concordance_index_censored (Harrell) — the latter is documented to bias upward at ≥49% censoring and you will exceed 90%. Bootstrap 1000 resamples AT THE PATIENT LEVEL for confidence intervals. Report against TRIPOD+AI; self-assess with PROBAST+AI. Benchmark to beat: your own expert-weighted composite — a learned model that does not clearly exceed it in PR-AUC AND calibration should not ship.
  - needs: Same ~30–100 events. Note honestly: with 30 events, a 95% CI on AUC is roughly ±0.12 — you can demonstrate 'plausibly useful', not 'validated'. Say so in the console UI and in any clinical conversation.
  - why: Row-level CV on person-day data leaks the same patient into train and test and will inflate every metric by an unquantifiable margin — this is the single most likely way this project produces a number that is wrong. Van Calster's 'no such thing as a validated prediction model' argues for continuous monitoring over one-time validation, which suits a deployed product better than a paper anyway. A systematic review of ML on remote-monitoring data found 73.7% of 76 studies at HIGH risk of bias, mainly from methodological shortcuts of exactly this kind.
- **LATER UPGRADE (only past ~1,000 patients / ~100 events) — landmark supervised model with nonlinear learner** — Landmark dataset: for each landmark day L ∈ {3, 7, 14, 21, 28} and each patient still event-free and under observation at L, emit ONE row with features summarizing the trailing 7 days (mean/slope/max of each EWMA z-score, composite index, CUSUM statistics, coverage) and label = event within the next 14 days. Fit a gradient-boosted model with a landmark indicator. This is the Bressman design and it is the only place a tree ensemble is defensible. via `lightgbm>=4.0 (Bressman used LightGBM; it beat logistic regression 0.82 vs 0.76 AUC and was better calibrated). Alternatively sksurv.ensemble.RandomSurvivalForest on the same landmark rows (min_samples_leaf raised to 15–20, max_depth 3–4). scikit-learn CalibratedClassifierCV(method='isotonic', cv=StratifiedGroupKFold(5)) on top.`
  - params: LightGBM starting point for small data: num_leaves=7, max_depth=3, min_child_samples=20, learning_rate=0.03, n_estimators tuned by early stopping on a patient-grouped validation fold, subsample=0.8, colsample_bytree=0.6, reg_lambda=5.0, is_unbalance=False (do not rebalance). Landmarks every 7 days; horizon 14 days. Cluster all landmark rows from the same patient into the same CV fold.
  - needs: ≥1,000 patients / ≥100 events before this outperforms the penalized discrete-time model in a way you can trust. Below that it will win in CV and lose in deployment.
  - why: Bressman et al. (n=215, post-discharge step counts) got AUC 0.82 with LightGBM vs 0.76 with logistic regression and better calibration — so the nonlinear gain is real but modest, and it appeared at n=215. The Shabani simulation, however, found landmark-LGBM only beat classical methods at n=1000–1500, not at n=300–650. Treat these as bracketing evidence: possible at a few hundred patients, reliable past a thousand.

## Open questions

- What is the actual complication incidence in your target procedures and windows? Everything in this report keys off it. TKA/THA 90-day surgical-site infection is typically ~1%, 90-day readmission ~5-8%, MUA after TKA ~2-5%. At 1% you need ~3,000 patients to reach 30 events; at 6% you need ~500. This single number determines whether the learned hazard model is 6 months away or 3 years away, and it should be settled before any modeling work is scheduled.
- Will you have adjudicated outcomes at all, or only self-report? A learned model needs a date and a type per event, adjudicated consistently. If your only outcome channel is patient conversational check-ins, your labels are both noisy and informatively missing (sick patients disengage from check-ins too), which compounds the censoring problem rather than solving it. Is there a path to EHR/claims linkage for outcome ascertainment?
- What is the real device adherence curve in your population? The COPD study reported 86% median adherence; the readmission study did not report it. Post-op orthopedic patients aged 65+ in the first two weeks after surgery may be materially worse. The shape of the dropout curve determines whether IPCW is a nice-to-have or the central methodological problem.
- Should the score's clinical target be 'any complication' or a specific one? A pooled outcome maximizes event count (essential at your scale) but produces a score that cannot tell a provider what to DO. A first-principles answer: pool for the model, then use the composite's driver decomposition (already implemented in composite.py) for the narrative — but this needs a clinical advisor's sign-off that the drivers map to plausible actions.
- What is the tolerable alert burden? Every threshold decision reduces to alerts-per-clinician-per-week. Without a number here you cannot choose an operating point, and AUC is not decision-relevant. BVS3's 74% sensitivity at 85% specificity implies a specific false-alarm rate; is 15% of well patients flagged acceptable in your care model?
- Is there any external cohort you could borrow to pre-train or at least sanity-check the baseline hazard shape? A published post-op complication hazard curve by day would let you fix the spline shape from literature and spend all your scarce events on the covariate coefficients — a substantial statistical win. Worth a targeted literature search for TKA/THA time-to-complication distributions.
- Does SQLite hold up for person-day materialization? 1,000 patients x 90 days = 90,000 rows is trivial, but if you move to intraday resolution or add per-metric rows, the Lambda + SQLite combination becomes a concern. This is the most likely concrete trigger for the Postgres migration.
- Regulatory posture: at what point does a calibrated probability of a specific complication cross from wellness/RTM into a clinical decision support claim requiring FDA consideration? The tiered, uncalibrated, narrative-framed output you have today sits in a materially different regulatory position from 'this patient has a 12% chance of infection in the next 14 days'. This should be answered BEFORE the calibration work, not after.

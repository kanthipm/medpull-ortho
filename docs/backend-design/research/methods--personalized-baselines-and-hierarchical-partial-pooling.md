# Personalized baselines and hierarchical / partial-pooling models when per-patient data is short — methods, parameters, Python stack, and data requirements for MedPull Recovery Copilot

## Summary

The single highest-value finding is that the exact problem you have — "the pre-op window is too short to trust a per-patient baseline" — has a closed-form, peer-reviewed, published-2025 solution in the clinical chemistry literature: parametric empirical Bayes (PEB) personalized reference intervals (Røys et al., Clinical Chemistry 2025, PMC12582661). It gives a shrinkage weight B_n = B1·n / (B1·n + (1 − B1)) that continuously interpolates from a pure population prior at n = 0 days of baseline to a pure personal baseline as n grows, and a matching alert threshold Z·sqrt(1 − B1·B_n)·σ_pop that automatically widens when the patient's baseline is short. It is arithmetic — no MCMC, no compiler, runs in microseconds inside a Lambda handler — and it subsumes your current SD-floor hack, your confidence gate, and the "no pre-op data" case as special cases of one formula. B1 is the intraclass correlation, and it connects directly to the clinical-chemistry index of individuality via B1 = 1/(1 + II²), which also yields the days-of-baseline formula n = R·II²/(1 − R) for target reliability R. On days-of-data: steps need 2–6 days for ICC ≥ 0.80 (Murphy 2026, Espin & Júdice 2026), sleep duration needs 3–7 nights, but sleep *variability* needs 41–65 nights and is a trap you should not ship on a 1–4 week pre-op window (Soon & Chee 2026, Sleep, from 3.7M person-nights); resting HR at an ICC of ~0.64 needs ~6 days for reliability 0.90 and ~11 for 0.95. For the estimation stack: statsmodels MixedLM and scikit-learn GaussianProcessRegressor are Lambda-safe (pure numpy/scipy, sub-100 ms at your n); PyMC/bambi are not (PyTensor needs a runtime C++ compiler and a writable cache) and NumPyro/JAX only fits in a container-image Lambda — both belong in a nightly batch job that writes posterior summaries to the DB. Two design warnings specific to you: an immediately-pre-op step baseline is biased low by pre-operative activity decline (Sensors 2026, PMC13259192, 238 TKA/THA patients), so your ratio-to-expected will look falsely good; and GLMM/mixed-model inference has type-I error up to 47.2% when within-cluster correlation varies across subgroups (Hyun 2026, BMC Med Res Methodol) — which is exactly the heteroscedastic-CVI failure mode the PEB paper itself reports (flagging blew out to 16.4% for cortisol).

## Findings

### Parametric empirical Bayes personalized reference intervals — the exact closed form you should implement
*[strong]*

Røys et al., Clinical Chemistry 2025 (PMC12582661). Two-level normal model: X_n | μ_I ~ iid N(μ_I, σ_I²); μ_I ~ iid N(μ_pop, σ_G²). Shrunk personal set point: Ŷ = μ_pop + (X̄_n − μ_pop)·B_n, with shrinkage factor B_n = σ_G² / (σ_G² + σ_I²/n), equivalently B_n = B1·n / (B1·n + (1 − B1)) where B1 = σ_G²/(σ_G² + σ_I²) is the ICC. Alert rule for the next observation: flag when |X_{n+1} − Ŷ| > Z·sqrt(1 − B1·B_n)·σ_pop, where σ_pop = sqrt(σ_G² + σ_I²). Limits: at n=0, B_n=0 and the rule collapses exactly to the population reference interval; as n→∞ it collapses to |X_{n+1} − X̄_n| > Z·σ_I. At n=1 it is a *narrower and more correct* reference change value than the classical RCV because it corrects for regression to the mean. Performance in the validation cohort (30 healthy adults, ~10 weekly samples each; parameters from a LIS of 1,986–185,488 samples/analyte, 2015–2023): flagging fell from 4.7%→0.3% (albumin), 5.4%→3.7% (phosphate), 7.1%→3.9% (cortisone) versus population intervals, and 12.4%→5.3% for serial 17-OHP pairs versus classical log-normal RCV. Code: R 4.4.1 with refineR 1.6.2 + robustbase 0.99.4.1, scripts at doi 10.5281/zenodo.15680355 — but the algorithm is four lines of numpy.

> Røys EÅ et al. A Parametric Empirical Bayes Approach to Personalized Reference Intervals and Reference Change Values. Clin Chem 2025. PMC12582661

### The PEB model's stated failure mode is exactly the one that will bite a wearable deployment: within-person variance heterogeneity
*[strong]*

The authors explicitly identify 'CVI heteroscedasticity, which violates the assumption of the PEB model' as the cause of degraded performance: cortisol flagged 16.4% and 17-hydroxyprogesterone 11.5% of healthy observations against a 5% target when biological-variation-study parameters were used, and 17-OHP went from 0.0% to 5.5% flagging under LIS parameters, 'attributed to heteroscedasticity'. Translation for you: a single global σ_I per metric is wrong. Patients with atrial fibrillation, beta-blockers, poor sensor contact, or shift work have far larger σ_I than the cohort mean, and a global σ_I will over-flag them. Other stated limitations: baseline data contaminated by pathological values 'distort individualized thresholds and misguide clinical interpretation'; parameter-source datasets should be ≥5,000 samples with the pathological fraction ≤30%; consecutive samples need ≥24 h separation to avoid autocorrelation (directly relevant — your daily aggregates are autocorrelated, intraday samples badly so).

> Røys EÅ et al. Clin Chem 2025, PMC12582661 (Limitations section)

### Index of individuality: the decision rule for whether a personal baseline is even worth computing per metric
*[strong]*

II = CV_I / CV_G (strictly sqrt(CV_A² + CV_I²)/CV_G, including analytical/device variation CV_A). Interpretation is standard: II < 0.6 = high individuality, population reference intervals are actively misleading and a personal baseline is mandatory; II > 1.4 = low individuality, the population interval is fine and a personal baseline adds nothing but variance; 0.6–1.4 = intermediate, use both. Empirical anchors: Kazar et al., Clin Chim Acta 2026 — II < 0.6 for liver enzymes, albumin, creatinine, ferritin and thyroid hormones; II > 1.4 for iron; 94.7% of relative-individuality values < 1. Wu et al., CCLM 2025 (peri-menopausal women) — most II between 0.6 and 1.4. Reference change value: RCV = sqrt(2)·Z·sqrt(CV_A² + CV_I²), with Z = 1.96 two-sided / 1.65 one-sided. The critical bridge to the PEB model: B1 = 1/(1 + II²). So II = 0.5 → B1 = 0.80; II = 0.75 → B1 = 0.64; II = 1.0 → B1 = 0.50. You should compute and publish an II per metric per procedure from your own cohort as soon as you have ~30 patients with ≥7 pre-op days.

> Kazar et al., Clin Chim Acta 2026; Wu et al., Clin Chem Lab Med 2025; Fernandes et al., Equine Vet J 2025 (PMID 39748488)

### How many baseline samples are needed for a personal set point — the formula, not a rule of thumb
*[strong]*

The classical Harris/Fraser result: to estimate an individual's homeostatic set point within ±D% with probability corresponding to Z, n = (Z·CV_within / D)². Published instantiations show the answer is entirely CV-driven and ranges over two orders of magnitude: QTc/QRS intervals need n = 1 'at a closeness of 10% with a 95% probability' (Wu et al., West J Emerg Med 2025, PMID 40795013); Røraas reports 'one sample is sufficient to estimate the homeostatic set points within ±15%' for several analytes; canine cortisol (II 1.14) needs 42 samples; canine SDMA needs 8 (90% CI) and 45 (95% CI), creatinine 2 and 12 (J Vet Intern Med 2018, PMID 29469955). Do NOT copy any single number — compute n = (1.96·CV_I / D)² per metric from your own cohort with D set by clinical relevance (e.g. D = 3 bpm on a 60 bpm RHR = 5%; if CV_I(RHR) ≈ 6%, n = (1.96·6/5)² ≈ 5.5 → 6 days).

> Wu et al. West J Emerg Med 2025 PMID 40795013; J Vet Intern Med 2018 PMID 29469955; Røraas et al. Clin Chim Acta 2017 PMID 28257883

### Reliability-based days-of-data via Spearman–Brown — and the derived numbers for resting HR
*[moderate]*

Spearman–Brown: to reach a target reliability R by averaging k days when the single-day ICC is ρ, k = R(1 − ρ) / (ρ(1 − R)). Substituting ρ = B1 = 1/(1 + II²) gives the clean identity n_days = R·II² / (1 − R). Anchor for HR: Takano et al., JMIR Form Res 2026 (methamphetamine cohort, 8-week wearable observation) report 'approximately 64% of the variance in heart rate was attributable to interindividual differences, while 12% reflected variability across events within the same individual' → ρ ≈ 0.64, II ≈ 0.75. That yields: R = 0.80 → 3 days; R = 0.90 → 6 days; R = 0.95 → 11 days. Caveat: N = 7 participants, so treat ρ = 0.64 as a placeholder to be re-estimated from your own cohort with a one-line MixedLM variance-components fit. Corroborating direction of travel: a COPD remote-monitoring study (JMIR mHealth 2024, N = 146, median 56.5 nights/patient, IQR 32–113) found that 'as the duration of night-sleep monitoring increased from 1 to 30 days, the statistical means of HRV, HR, and respiration showed a better reflection of an individual's health condition' — i.e. more than a week keeps helping for HRV.

> Takano et al., JMIR Form Res 2026, PMC12954691; JMIR mHealth uHealth 2024 (COPD continuous HRV/respiration study, N=146)

### Days needed for step/activity baselines — hard ICC-based numbers
*[strong]*

Murphy et al., JMIR mHealth uHealth 2026 (PMID 41666367; N = 149 short-term, N = 64 medium-term): '2 to 6 days depending on the physical activity cluster' for ICC ≥ 0.80 within a 7-day window, but '6 to 11 days' when the target is a 28-day-representative estimate. Espin & Júdice, Scand J Med Sci Sports 2026 (N = 106): 2 days for sit-to-stand transitions, 3 days for sedentary/sitting/lying time, 4 days for stepping time, standing time and step count (ICC ≥ 0.85). Letts et al., Pediatr Exerc Sci 2026 (N = 109): '4 days of 6 hours of wear balances a 0.7 reliability threshold'. Danilevicz et al., Eur Rev Aging Phys Act 2024: rest-activity rhythm metrics need 2–3 days (transition probabilities) to 5 days (inter-daily stability), ICC ≥ 0.75. Practical rule for MedPull: 4–5 valid pre-op days is the floor for a step baseline; 7 is comfortable; 11+ if you want it to represent a whole month.

> Murphy et al. JMIR Mhealth Uhealth 2026 PMID 41666367; Espin & Júdice Scand J Med Sci Sports 2026 PMID 42436015; Letts et al. Pediatr Exerc Sci 2026 PMID 42413906; Danilevicz et al. Eur Rev Aging Phys Act 2024 PMID 39427121

### Sleep duration needs 3–7 nights; sleep VARIABILITY needs 41–65 nights — do not ship a sleep-regularity feature
*[strong]*

Soon & Chee, Sleep 2026;49(6):zsag083 (PMC13266552), reviewing a reference study of 3.7 million person-nights from >10,000 individuals with 363 nights per participant (WHOOP): mean sleep duration is reliably captured 'in as few as 3 to 7 nights', but sleep-variability measures computed from 7 days 'showed correlations of only 0.50–0.58 with their corresponding reference values', 2 weeks gives 0.61–0.67, 'still well below the acceptable reliability threshold of 0.80' — 6–10 weeks (41–65 nights) is required. A 7-night total-sleep-time estimate 'could differ from the true value by up to 50 min in either direction', and sleep-onset-variability limits of agreement 'spanned over 2 h'. Women and younger adults need 'up to 30 additional days' for reliable sleep-fragmentation variability. Direct implication: your sleep-duration EWMA is defensible on a 7-day pre-op window; any variability/regularity-derived feature computed on the same window is noise and will manufacture false alerts.

> Soon & Chee. Avoiding variability in sleep variability assessments: how many nights are enough? Sleep 2026;49(6):zsag083, PMC13266552

### An immediately-pre-op activity baseline is biased LOW — this breaks ratio-to-expected recovery curves
*[strong]*

Sensors 2026, PMC13259192: 'Preoperative Decline and Postoperative Recovery of Wearable-Derived Physical Activity over a Four-Year Perioperative Period in Total Knee and Hip Arthroplasty', N = 238 (147 TKA, 91 THA), analysed with piecewise linear mixed-effects models, explicitly contrasting 'activity measured immediately before surgery and a more remote baseline approximating longer-term habitual activity'. Patients decline for months before arthroplasty. If your f(d) ratio-to-expected divides post-op steps by a baseline captured in the last 1–2 weeks pre-op, the denominator is already depressed by disease, so recovery ratios are inflated and you will systematically under-alert on poor recovery. Two fixes: (a) prefer a remote baseline (e.g. the 90th percentile of daily steps over the 3–6 months before surgery) when available; (b) if only a short window exists, scale it by a procedure-specific pre-op decline factor estimated from the cohort, and label the baseline provenance in the API response.

> Sensors (Basel) 2026, PMC13259192, N=238 TKA/THA, piecewise linear mixed-effects models

### Field-realistic pre-op collection windows and the clinical value of return-to-baseline activity
*[moderate]*

Observed practice in comparable studies: 'up to four weeks pre-operatively' for TKA (PLoS One 2026, PMC13082708, up to 160 participants, longitudinal step-count trend analysis); 'at least 5 days of preoperative' data as an inclusion requirement (Aesthet Surg J Open Forum 2025, PMC12635626, 21 of 51 enrolled with complete data — note the 41% completion rate, a realistic adherence expectation); '1-week period prior to surgery' (Comput Methods Programs Biomed 2025, PMC13249102, N = 65 older adults before major noncardiac surgery). Outcome evidence that ratio-to-baseline activity carries real signal: a review of continuous wearable monitoring after colorectal surgery (Diagnostics 2025, PMC12428718; 778 patients across nine studies, median wear time 6 days) found 'Low return-to-baseline activity on the day before discharge independently predicted any complication (OR 0.60 per 10% increment)'. This is the closest published effect size to your composite deviation index and is worth reproducing as a validation target.

> PLoS One 2026 PMC13082708; Aesthet Surg J Open Forum 2025 PMC12635626; Diagnostics 2025 PMC12428718

### statsmodels MixedLM — what it does and, critically, what it cannot do
*[strong]*

Fits Y = Xβ + Zγ + Q₁η₁ + … + Q_kη_k + ε via Newton–Raphson plus EM (Lindstrom & Bates 1988), ML or REML. Supports: random intercepts, random slopes, explicit variance components, nested groups. Does NOT support: non-Gaussian responses (no GLMM), structured residual covariance (no AR(1) within group — a real limitation for daily wearable series, which are autocorrelated), and fully crossed random effects (the documented workaround is to treat the entire dataset as one group, which is impractical). BLUPs are exposed as the `random_effects` attribute of the fitted result — these ARE the empirical-Bayes shrunk per-patient intercepts you want, so MixedLM gives you both the variance components (σ_G² from the RE covariance, σ_I² from `scale`) and the shrunk personal baselines in one fit. Known pathologies: singular covariance matrices and variance parameters estimated at exactly zero, which happens routinely when the number of patients is small or the within-patient series are short — guard for it and fall back to the population prior.

> https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/mixed_linear.html

### Mixed-model inference is fragile with few clusters and heterogeneous within-cluster correlation
*[moderate]*

Hyun et al., BMC Med Res Methodol 2026: standard GLMM analyses 'can lead to severely elevated type 1 error rates of up to 47.2% compared to the 5% nominal level if the within-cluster correlation varies across subgroups' — with 10–1000 patients whose device quality, medication and comorbidity differ wildly, this is your default state, not an edge case. Ouyang et al., Stat Methods Med Res 2024: the CR3 cluster-robust variance estimator 'could be slightly conservative when the number of clusters was below 16'. Lee & Cheung, Trials 2024: 'For settings with a small number of clusters, Kenward-Roger and Satterthwaite small-sample corrections are applied', but 'mixed-effects models with a Satterthwaite or Kenward-Roger small sample correction can still result in inflated or overly conservative type I error rates, respectively'. Classical simulation guidance (Maas & Hox, Methodology 2005) is that fixed effects are fine with ~30 groups but level-2 variance standard errors are underestimated by roughly 15% below ~50 groups. Practical consequence: below ~50 patients, do not report p-values or confidence intervals on variance components; use the point estimates only as inputs to the shrinkage weight, and prefer a weakly-informative Bayesian fit if you need honest uncertainty.

> Hyun et al. BMC Med Res Methodol 2026; Ouyang et al. Stat Methods Med Res 2024; Lee & Cheung, Trials 2024; Maas & Hox, Methodology 2005;1(3):86-92

### scikit-learn GaussianProcessRegressor is adequate and Lambda-safe at your per-patient scale — but only per-patient
*[strong]*

Documented properties: not sparse, uses the full covariance matrix, O(n³) time and O(n²) memory, single-output only, 'no hierarchical/tree structures — flat kernel matrix required'. Hyperparameters optimised by maximising the log-marginal-likelihood with L-BFGS-B and analytic gradients; `n_restarts_optimizer` re-runs from random draws within the kernel bounds to escape local optima; `alpha` adds Tikhonov regularisation to the diagonal (or use a WhiteKernel to learn the noise); `normalize_y=True` sets the prior mean to the training mean rather than zero. At n ≤ 365 daily points per patient, n³ ≈ 5·10⁷ flops — tens of milliseconds, entirely fine inside a Lambda invocation. What it cannot do is share information across patients: there is no multi-task or hierarchical GP in scikit-learn, so a GP gives you a smooth personal trajectory with honest uncertainty bands but zero partial pooling. Use the GP for the trajectory display and the PEB/MixedLM machinery for the pooling; do not try to make the GP do both.

> https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/gaussian_process.html

### pyGAM is Lambda-safe but is NOT a GAMM — it has no random effects
*[strong]*

pyGAM provides LinearGAM, LogisticGAM, PoissonGAM, GammaGAM and ExpectileGAM with term types s() (spline), f() (factor), l() (linear), te() (tensor), fit by penalised IRLS with a gridsearch over the smoothing penalty `lam`. Crucially for deployment it 'is tested on Python 3.10+ and depends on NumPy, SciPy, and progressbar2' — a tiny dependency footprint that fits comfortably in a Lambda zip. But it has no random-effects/mixed-model support, so it cannot fit a true GAMM. Verify the numeric defaults against your installed version with `LinearGAM().get_params()` rather than trusting documentation (commonly n_splines=20, spline_order=3, lam=0.6). The practical GAMM substitute in Python: construct a penalised cubic-regression-spline basis for day-since-surgery with patsy (`cr(day, df=5)`) or scipy, put those columns in as fixed effects, and fit with statsmodels MixedLM with a per-patient random intercept and random slope — this is Wood's spline-as-random-effect equivalence done by hand, and it runs in Lambda.

> https://pygam.readthedocs.io/en/latest/ ; Servén D. & Brummitt C. (2018) pyGAM, Zenodo doi 10.5281/zenodo.1208723

### Bambi/PyMC are batch-only; NumPyro needs a container image — neither belongs in the request path
*[moderate]*

Bambi is 'a high-level Bayesian model-building interface written in Python' layered on PyMC, with automatically scaled weakly-informative default priors (the documented linear-regression example assigns Intercept ~ Normal(298.5, 261.0) and the slope ~ Normal(0, 48.9), i.e. priors derived from the observed data scale), tested on Python 3.12+. The deployment blocker is PyMC's PyTensor backend, which compiles C code at runtime and needs a C++ toolchain plus a writable compile cache — an AWS Lambda zip runtime has neither a compiler nor a writable filesystem outside /tmp, and falling back to the pure-Python backend makes NUTS unusably slow. NumPyro/JAX has no compiler dependency but the jaxlib CPU wheel plus scipy/pandas will exceed the 250 MB unzipped Lambda zip limit, so it requires a container-image Lambda (10 GB limit), and JIT compilation of the NUTS kernel costs seconds on every cold start. Correct architecture for 10–1000 patients: fit hierarchical models nightly in AWS Batch/Fargate or a container Lambda, persist posterior summaries (μ_pop per stratum, σ_G, σ_I, per-patient shrunk means) to the database, and let the synchronous request path do nothing but arithmetic on those stored numbers.

> https://bambinos.github.io/bambi/ (backend, priors, Python version); PyTensor/JAX packaging constraints inferred from documented AWS Lambda limits

### Replace the SD floor with a moderated (empirical-Bayes) variance estimate
*[moderate]*

Your SD floor is an ad-hoc variance regulariser and it is the right *instinct* with the wrong *functional form*: a floor is discontinuous, applies equally to a patient with 3 days and one with 30, and does nothing about a variance that is over-estimated. The principled version is a scaled-inverse-χ² prior on the within-person variance, giving the moderated estimator σ̂²_i = (ν₀·σ̄²_pop + (n_i − 1)·s_i²) / (ν₀ + n_i − 1). ν₀ is the prior strength in pseudo-observations; ν₀ = 8 is a sensible default (roughly 'the population variance is worth about 8 days of this patient's data'), and ν₀ can itself be estimated from the cohort by matching the observed dispersion of the s_i² to a scaled-χ² — this is precisely the moderated-variance trick from limma (Smyth, Stat Appl Genet Mol Biol 2004;3:Article3), which was designed for exactly your situation: thousands of units each with a handful of replicates. Keep the hard floor underneath as a degenerate-case guard (a device reporting a constant value gives s_i = 0 and would otherwise produce infinite z-scores), but set it at a device-resolution-derived value (e.g. 0.5 bpm for RHR, 0.05 °C for skin temperature) rather than a clinically-chosen one, so it fires only on sensor pathology.

> Smyth GK, Stat Appl Genet Mol Biol 2004;3:Article3 (moderated variance / empirical Bayes); Røys et al. Clin Chem 2025 for the mean-shrinkage analogue

### Skew and heteroscedasticity: transform before z-scoring, because your alerts live in the upper tail
*[moderate]*

The PEB framework itself 'assumes log-normality (or Box-Cox transformability); failure of data transformation could yield invalid parameters'. HRV in particular is analysed as LnRMSSD essentially universally in the applied literature (e.g. Abruñedo-Lombardero et al., Sensors 2025 use 'the natural logarithm of the root mean square of successive differences (LnRMSSD)'; Esco et al., Sensors 2025 recommend 'weekly averages and the coefficient of variation (CV)' of routine near-daily HRV rather than isolated measures). Honest caveat: I could not surface a paper in this session that states 'RMSSD is log-normal' in so many words, so treat log-normality as strong convention plus a testable hypothesis, not a citation-backed fact — estimate λ empirically per metric with scipy.stats.boxcox_normmax(x, method='mle') on pooled pre-op residuals and freeze it per cohort version. Practical asymmetry that makes this matter: a right-skewed metric z-scored on the raw scale generates far more than the nominal rate of high-side flags and far fewer low-side ones, so a nominally 2.66-sigma EWMA chart on raw RMSSD is not running at the false-alarm rate you think it is. Use Yeo-Johnson (sklearn PowerTransformer(method='yeo-johnson')) for metrics with zeros or signed values such as temperature deltas.

> Røys et al. Clin Chem 2025 (transformation assumption); Abruñedo-Lombardero et al. Sensors 2025; Esco et al. Sensors 2025 PMID 41516438

### Robust location and scale: which estimator, which constant, which library function
*[strong]*

Median/MAD: σ̂ = 1.4826 × MAD is the Gaussian-consistent scale estimate; MAD has a 50% breakdown point but only ~37% asymptotic efficiency at the Gaussian, so on a 7-day baseline window it is noticeably noisier than the SD. Huber's M-estimator with tuning constant c = 1.345 achieves ~95% efficiency at the Gaussian while bounding the influence of outliers, and is the better default for short windows. The Rousseeuw–Croux Qn estimator gives ~58% efficiency at the same 50% breakdown and is a strict improvement on MAD when you have ≥10 points. Python: scipy.stats.median_abs_deviation(x, scale='normal') applies the 1.4826 constant for you; statsmodels.robust.scale.mad, statsmodels.robust.scale.huber (Huber's Proposal 2, joint location and scale) and statsmodels.robust.scale.qn_scale are all pure numpy/scipy and Lambda-safe. Recommendation: robust *location* (median) always for the baseline centre, because a single post-op day leaking into a pre-op window shifts a mean badly; robust *scale* via Huber, then moderated toward the population variance as above.

> scipy.stats.median_abs_deviation and statsmodels.robust.scale documentation; Rousseeuw & Croux, JASA 1993 (Qn/Sn); Huber (1964) tuning constant 1.345

### Deployed wearable-detection systems use per-person baselines of weeks, not days, and report modest real-world performance
*[strong]*

Calibration for what is achievable: Mishra et al., Nat Biomed Eng 2020 (PMC9020268), 32 infected from a 5,300-person cohort, two-tiered warning on extreme elevations in resting heart rate relative to each individual's baseline — '63% of COVID-19 cases could have been detected before symptom onset', 22 detected at or before onset, four at least nine days earlier. Commercial Oura deployment (npj Digit Med 2024, PMC11116498, 8,000+ participants): ROC AUC 0.777 with participant-specific baseline correction, early detection improving from 3.5 to 4.1 days. Oura fever detection (Sensors 2024, PMC10975930, 16,794 valid ground-truth days): AUROC 0.85, average precision 0.25, and at 50% sensitivity a 0.8% false-positive rate. Influenza challenge study (J Infect Dis 2023, PMC9384446, N = 20, semi-supervised multivariable anomaly model trained on pre-inoculation data): 16/17 (94%) presymptomatic/asymptomatic cases identified, 23 h before symptoms — but note the model was trained on a clean, controlled pre-exposure baseline, which is the best case and not your case. Systematic review (Lancet Digit Health 2022, PMC9020803): AUC across models 0.52–0.92, presymptomatic detection 20%–88%. The lesson for your risk tiers: even with far more data than you will have, the honest ceiling is AUC ~0.78–0.85 with average precision ~0.25 for rare events, and average precision — not AUC — is the metric to report when prevalence is single-digit percent.

> Mishra et al. Nat Biomed Eng 2020 PMC9020268; npj Digit Med 2024 PMC11116498; Sensors 2024 PMC10975930; J Infect Dis 2023 PMC9384446; Lancet Digit Health 2022 PMC9020803


## Implications for backend

- Refactor `baseline.py` around a single PEB function `shrunk_baseline(x_valid, mu_pop, sigma_g2, sigma_i2) -> (Y_hat, threshold_sd, B_n)` and make every downstream consumer (deviation.py, composite.py, confidence.py, risk.py) read from it. Today's SD floor, the >=3-of-6 coverage gate and the <40%-of-7-days low-confidence rule are three separate ad-hoc encodings of the same quantity — the shrinkage weight B_n — and collapsing them into one number that is both computed and displayed removes an entire class of inconsistency (a patient can currently pass the coverage gate while having a baseline built from 3 days).
- Persist a per-metric, per-procedure-stratum prior table in the database — (metric, procedure, age_band, sex, bmi_band) -> (mu_pop, sigma_g2, sigma_i2, B1, lambda_transform, prior_version) — populated nightly by a batch job and read by Lambda. This makes the request path pure arithmetic (microseconds), makes every alert reproducible from a versioned prior, and gives you the audit trail a clinical reviewer will demand. Version the table and never mutate a row in place: an alert issued last month must be re-derivable.
- Add a `baseline_provenance` field to the patient/metric record with values like PRE_OP_N_DAYS=7, POST_OP_ANCHORED_POD5_14, or POPULATION_ONLY, and surface B_n as a 0–1 `baseline_confidence` in the API and UI. When B_n < 0.4 the correct clinical message is 'this patient is being judged mostly against people like them, not against themselves' — that is a fundamentally different claim from 'this patient deviated from their own normal', and the narrative LLM must be given the distinction explicitly so it does not overstate personalisation.
- Insert a transform stage before all z-scoring: ln(RMSSD), ln(1+steps), frozen per-metric Box-Cox lambdas stored in the same versioned prior table. This changes the numeric meaning of every existing threshold (EWMA L=2.66, composite high>2.0 / elevated>1.2, CUSUM k=0.5/h=5.0), so the transform migration and a threshold recalibration must ship together, with the golden-tier tests regenerated in the same commit. Do not ship the transform alone.
- Change the EWMA/CUSUM control-limit derivation to consume the PEB threshold sd = sqrt(1 - B1*B_n)*sigma_pop instead of a floored per-patient SD. This makes the charts automatically wider (fewer alerts) for patients with short baselines and tighter as baseline accumulates — the behaviour you want — without any special-case branching. Raise the effective alert Z to ~3.0: at 6 metrics x 365 days, a nominal 1.96 gives roughly 110 flags per patient-year, which will destroy clinician trust before the first outcome label ever arrives.
- Move all model fitting (MixedLM variance components, spline recovery curves, any NumPyro hierarchical fit) into a nightly batch job — AWS Batch/Fargate or a container-image Lambda — that writes only summary parameters to the DB. PyMC/bambi cannot run in a Lambda zip runtime at all (PyTensor needs a runtime C++ compiler and a writable cache), and jax/NumPyro exceeds the 250 MB unzipped limit and pays seconds of JIT on cold start. statsmodels MixedLM, scikit-learn GaussianProcessRegressor, pyGAM inference and all robust-scale functions are pure numpy/scipy and are safe to call synchronously.
- Fix the step/walking-speed denominator: the current ratio-to-expected almost certainly divides by a pre-op baseline that is depressed by pre-operative activity decline, which inflates recovery ratios and systematically under-alerts on poor recovery. Either use a remote habitual baseline (90th percentile of daily steps over the 3-6 months pre-op) when the data exists, or multiply the short-window baseline by a procedure-specific decline correction factor, and record which was used in `baseline_provenance`.
- Replace the fixed composite weights (RHR .25, temp .25, HRV .20, RR .15, steps .15) with inverse-variance weights derived from the same variance components: w_m proportional to 1/(1 - B1_m*B_n,m), normalised. Metrics whose baseline is short or whose within-person variance is large should contribute less to the composite automatically. Keep the current fixed weights as the fallback when the prior table is unavailable, and log which weighting produced each score.
- Instrument for the metric that will actually matter: with single-digit-percent event rates, report average precision / PR-AUC and alerts-per-patient-week, not AUC or accuracy. The comparable published ceilings are AUROC 0.78-0.85 with average precision around 0.25 on cohorts of thousands; any internal number materially better than that on tens of patients is leakage or optimism, not signal.
- Add a per-patient heteroscedasticity guard. The PEB paper's own worst failures (16.4% flagging for cortisol) came from within-person variance varying across individuals, and the GLMM literature shows type-I error reaching 47.2% under the same condition. Concretely: compute each patient's observed s_i against the cohort sigma_i, and if s_i > 2*sigma_i, widen that patient's thresholds proportionally and drop their confidence tier rather than letting them generate a stream of alerts.

## Recommended stack

- **Per-patient baseline mean and alert threshold for every vital (RHR, HRV RMSSD, skin temp, SpO2, RR, sleep duration) — replaces the raw pre-op mean/SD and the confidence gate with one continuous formula** — Parametric empirical Bayes shrinkage (Røys et al. 2025). Compute the shrunk set point Ŷ_i = μ_pop,stratum + (X̄_i,n − μ_pop,stratum)·B_n with B_n = B1·n/(B1·n + 1 − B1), and flag when |x_today − Ŷ_i| > Z·sqrt(1 − B1·B_n)·σ_pop. Store B_n on the patient record and surface it in the API as `baseline_confidence`. via `numpy only (closed form, ~10 lines). Variance components from statsmodels>=0.14 MixedLM. Optional scipy>=1.11 for the transform step.`
  - params: Per metric, per procedure stratum: B1 = 1/(1+II²), initialised at B1 = 0.64 for RHR (II≈0.75), 0.50 for HRV (assume II≈1.0 until measured), 0.55 for skin temperature, 0.45 for RR, 0.70 for sleep duration; σ_pop and σ_I from the cohort MixedLM. n = number of VALID baseline days (≥18 h wear). Z = 3.0 for the primary alert (not 1.96 — at 6 metrics × 365 days, Z=1.96 yields ~110 nominal flags/patient/year; Z=3.0 yields ~6). Use Z = 2.0 for an amber/watch tier. Apply after the metric-specific transform (ln for RMSSD).
  - needs: To ship with published/borrowed priors: 0 patients. To estimate B1, σ_G and σ_I from your own data honestly: ≥30 patients with ≥7 valid pre-op days each (≈210 patient-days per metric); ≥50 patients before you quote any uncertainty on the variance components (Maas & Hox). Below 30 patients, hard-code priors from the literature and label them as such.
  - why: It is the only method found that degrades gracefully and correctly from zero baseline days to many, is closed-form (Lambda-safe, deterministic, unit-testable, no sampler), corrects for regression to the mean at n=1 (which a classical RCV does not), and has published false-positive-rate validation: flagging rates moved to within ~1 point of the 5% nominal target across five analytes while cutting one from 4.7% to 0.3%.
- **Estimating the population prior μ_pop stratified by age/sex/BMI/procedure, plus the variance components σ_G² and σ_I² that feed the shrinkage weight — and producing shrunk per-patient baselines for free** — Linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept per patient, fit by REML, on pre-op (or post-op-anchored) daily observations. BLUPs from the fit ARE the empirical-Bayes shrunk baselines. via `statsmodels>=0.14 (`statsmodels.formula.api.mixedlm`). Pure numpy/scipy — installs in a Lambda zip and fits in well under a second at your scale.`
  - params: `mixedlm("value_t ~ 1 + C(procedure) + age_c + C(sex) + bmi_c", data=df, groups=df.patient_id, re_formula="1").fit(reml=True, method=['lbfgs','powell'])`. Centre age and BMI at the cohort mean. σ_G² = `result.cov_re.iloc[0,0]`; σ_I² = `result.scale`; B1 = σ_G²/(σ_G²+σ_I²); shrunk baselines = `result.random_effects`. Guard: if σ_G² < 1e-8 (singular/boundary fit, common with few patients), set B1 = 0 and fall back to the population mean. Refit nightly, version the output, never in the request path.
  - needs: ≥30 patients × ≥5 days for a usable σ_G² point estimate; ≥50 patients before reporting uncertainty on it; below 20 patients the RE variance will frequently collapse to zero and you should not fit at all — use literature CV_I/CV_G instead.
  - why: One fit yields every number the PEB formula needs plus the stratified population prior, using a library that is already a plausible dependency and that runs anywhere. MixedLM's `random_effects` are exactly the partial-pooling estimates, so you get the shrinkage machinery without writing a sampler.
- **Regularising the per-patient within-person SD — the principled replacement for the current SD floor** — Moderated (empirical-Bayes) variance: σ̂²_i = (ν₀·σ̄²_pop + (n_i−1)·s_i²)/(ν₀ + n_i − 1), where s_i is a ROBUST scale estimate of the patient's baseline window. via `scipy>=1.11 (`scipy.stats.median_abs_deviation(x, scale='normal')`) and statsmodels>=0.14 (`statsmodels.robust.scale.huber`, `statsmodels.robust.scale.qn_scale`).`
  - params: ν₀ = 8 pseudo-observations as the default prior strength (tune by matching the cohort dispersion of s_i² to a scaled-χ²). Robust scale: Huber Proposal 2 with tuning c = 1.345 when n_i ≥ 5; median_abs_deviation with scale='normal' (constant 1.4826) when n_i < 5. Retain a hard floor at device resolution only: 0.5 bpm (RHR), 0.03 in ln-RMSSD units, 0.05 °C (skin temp), 0.3% (SpO2), 0.4 breaths/min (RR), 12 min (sleep). Baseline CENTRE should be the median, not the mean.
  - needs: Works at n_i = 1 (pure prior). Needs ≥30 patients to estimate σ̄²_pop and ν₀ from your own cohort; before that, set σ̄²_pop from published within-subject CVs and ν₀ = 8 by fiat.
  - why: A hard SD floor is discontinuous, ignores n_i, and cannot correct an over-estimated variance; the moderated estimator does all three and is the same estimator that made limma the standard for thousands-of-units/few-replicates inference. Huber at c=1.345 keeps 95% Gaussian efficiency — critical on a 5–7 day window where MAD's 37% efficiency would cost you real detection power — while still bounding a single contaminated day.
- **Non-linear population recovery curve for steps and walking speed — replacing the hand-tuned logistic f(d) = floor + (1−floor)/(1+exp(−r(d−d50)))** — Penalised-spline mixed model ('GAMM by hand'): a cubic regression spline basis in day-since-surgery as fixed effects, plus a per-patient random intercept and random slope on log(day+1). Fit offline, persist coefficients, evaluate in Lambda with a dot product. via `formulaic or patsy (`cr(day, df=5)`) for the basis + statsmodels>=0.14 MixedLM. If you only need the population curve with no patient random effects, pyGAM (LinearGAM) is a lighter alternative — but note it has NO random-effects support and therefore is not a GAMM.`
  - params: Basis: `cr(day, df=5)` with knots at post-op days 3, 7, 14, 28, 56 (place knots where recovery actually bends, not at quantiles). Model: `mixedlm("log1p(steps) ~ cr(day, df=5)*C(procedure) + age_c + C(sex) + bmi_c", groups=patient_id, re_formula="1 + np.log1p(day)").fit(reml=True)`. pyGAM alternative: `LinearGAM(s(0, n_splines=8, spline_order=3, constraints='monotonic_inc') + f(1)).gridsearch(X, y, lam=np.logspace(-3, 3, 11))`. Always confirm defaults with `LinearGAM().get_params()`.
  - needs: ≥40 patients per procedure with ≥30 post-op days each for a 5-df spline (rule of thumb: ≥8 patients per spline degree of freedom). Below that, keep the logistic but fit its 3 parameters (floor, r, d50) by nonlinear least squares per procedure and shrink each patient's implied offset toward it — do not fit a spline to 15 patients.
  - why: Your fixed logistic imposes a shape you have not validated and has no per-patient flexibility, so every deviation from that shape is attributed to the patient. A penalised spline lets the data pick the shape while the random slope lets each patient have their own recovery rate, partially pooled toward the procedure mean. Piecewise linear mixed-effects models are the published state of practice in exactly this domain (238 TKA/THA patients, Sensors 2026).
- **Smooth per-patient trajectory with an honest uncertainty band, for the clinician-facing chart and for interpolating irregular/missing days** — Gaussian process regression, one independent GP per patient per metric, fit on demand. via `scikit-learn>=1.4 (`sklearn.gaussian_process.GaussianProcessRegressor`). Do NOT reach for GPyTorch — it pulls in torch (≈900 MB), gives you nothing at n<1000, and is unjustifiable in this deployment.`
  - params: `kernel = ConstantKernel(1.0, (1e-2, 1e2)) * Matern(length_scale=7.0, length_scale_bounds=(3.0, 30.0), nu=1.5) + WhiteKernel(noise_level=sigma_i**2, noise_level_bounds=(1e-3, 1e1))`; `GaussianProcessRegressor(kernel=kernel, normalize_y=True, alpha=1e-6, n_restarts_optimizer=3, random_state=0)`. Fit on (day, value) with the day axis in days. nu=1.5 deliberately, not RBF: physiology is not infinitely differentiable and an RBF kernel over-smooths precisely the step changes you are trying to detect. The 3-day lower bound on length_scale stops the GP from interpolating daily noise and reporting a spuriously tight band.
  - needs: ≥10 observations per patient before a GP is meaningful; below that the marginal-likelihood optimiser will chase the bounds. With <10 points, plot the shrunk PEB mean with its band instead.
  - why: Cost is O(n³) with n ≤ 365 → tens of milliseconds, entirely acceptable inline in a Lambda handler; it produces a calibrated posterior SD at every day, including days with no data, which is exactly what your confidence gate should be consuming instead of a coverage count. Caveat to hold firmly: sklearn's GP has no multi-task or hierarchical support ('no hierarchical/tree structures — flat kernel matrix required'), so it does zero partial pooling. Use it for display and interpolation, never as the source of the baseline.
- **Full Bayesian hierarchical model — partially pooled per-patient recovery curve parameters and per-patient variances — once the cohort is large enough to justify it** — Hierarchical non-linear model with non-centred parameterisation, NUTS sampling, run as a NIGHTLY BATCH JOB, never in the request path. via `numpyro>=0.15 on jax[cpu] (preferred: no runtime C compiler needed), or bambi>=0.14 on pymc>=5.16 for the purely linear sub-models. Deploy in AWS Batch/Fargate or a container-image Lambda; the jax+scipy+pandas footprint exceeds the 250 MB unzipped Lambda zip limit.`
  - params: NUTS: 4 chains × 1000 warmup × 1000 draws, target_accept_prob=0.9, max_tree_depth=10. Priors for the logistic recovery curve: floor ~ Beta(2, 5); r ~ LogNormal(log(0.15), 0.5); d50 ~ TruncatedNormal(21, 10, low=1); per-patient offsets non-centred, z_i ~ Normal(0,1) with the offset = τ·z_i and τ ~ HalfNormal(0.3); observation noise σ ~ HalfNormal(1.0) on the log scale. Diagnostics gate before publishing: all R-hat < 1.01, ESS_bulk > 400 per parameter, zero divergences. Bambi equivalent for the linear case: `bmb.Model("value ~ 1 + C(procedure) + age_c + (1|patient_id)", df).fit(draws=1000, tune=1000, target_accept=0.9)` — its default priors are auto-scaled weakly informative (e.g. Intercept ~ Normal(data_mean, ~data_sd), slopes ~ Normal(0, scaled)), which is acceptable but you should override them explicitly for anything clinical.
  - needs: ≥50 patients before the hierarchical variance τ is identified at all; ≥100 with ≥30 post-op days each before per-patient curve parameters are worth reporting to a clinician. Below 50 patients this will sample fine and produce a posterior that is essentially your prior — which is misleading if you present it as learned.
  - why: This is the only route to honest uncertainty on the variance components at small N, and it lets each patient's recovery-curve parameters be partially pooled rather than either fixed at the procedure default or fit independently. It is also strictly optional: for 10–1000 patients the PEB closed form gives you ~90% of the value at ~1% of the operational complexity. Do this second, not first.
- **Making z-scores valid on skewed, heteroscedastic metrics (HRV especially) before any control chart or composite index touches them** — Fit a per-metric monotone transform on pooled cohort baseline data, freeze it as a versioned constant, and apply it as the first step of the pipeline. via `scipy>=1.11 (`scipy.stats.boxcox_normmax(x, method='mle')`, `scipy.stats.yeojohnson`) or scikit-learn>=1.4 (`sklearn.preprocessing.PowerTransformer(method='yeo-johnson', standardize=False)`).`
  - params: HRV RMSSD: use ln(RMSSD) — λ=0 by convention and by universal practice in the applied literature; verify with boxcox_normmax and only deviate if the MLE λ is outside [−0.2, 0.2]. Steps: ln(1+steps). Sleep duration, RHR, RR, SpO2: estimate λ per metric from ≥500 pooled baseline observations, then ROUND to the nearest 0.5 and freeze it in a config constant with a version tag. Skin temperature deviation (signed, can be negative): Yeo-Johnson. Re-estimate λ at most annually, and treat a change in λ as a breaking change to every stored z-score and threshold.
  - needs: ≥500 pooled baseline observations per metric (≈70 patients × 7 days) to estimate λ stably. Below that, use ln for RMSSD and steps and leave the rest untransformed — a wrong λ is worse than λ=1.
  - why: A right-skewed metric z-scored on the raw scale produces far more than the nominal rate of high-side exceedances and far fewer low-side ones, so a 2.66-sigma EWMA chart on raw RMSSD is not running at the false-alarm rate you believe. The PEB model explicitly 'assumes log-normality (or Box-Cox transformability)' and its own worst failures (16.4% flagging for cortisol) were attributed to unmodelled heteroscedasticity. Freezing λ rather than re-fitting per patient keeps thresholds comparable across patients and across releases.

## Open questions

- I could not surface, in this session, a paper that states in so many words that RMSSD is log-normally distributed — the practice of using LnRMSSD is near-universal in the applied literature but the distributional claim is usually assumed rather than cited. Before hard-coding ln(RMSSD), run scipy.stats.boxcox_normmax on your own pooled pre-op RMSSD and confirm the MLE lambda is near 0. If it is not, the convention is wrong for your device/population.
- There is no published number for how many days of wearable data are needed for a stable resting-HR, HRV or wrist-temperature baseline that is as clean as the sleep and physical-activity numbers. The ICC≈0.64 for HR comes from a 7-participant study. The single highest-value internal analysis you can run in week one is a variance-components MixedLM on whatever pre-op data you already have, per metric, to get your own ICC and hence your own required-days number — it costs one afternoon and replaces every borrowed constant in this report.
- Wrist skin temperature has no reliable published within-person CV that I could locate, and it is one of your two highest-weighted composite inputs. It is also the metric most confounded by ambient temperature, bedding and wear position. Consider whether it should carry 0.25 weight before you have measured its within-person variance and its day-to-day autocorrelation.
- The exact post-op window to use for a post-op-anchored baseline when no pre-op data exists is not established in the literature. POD 5-14 is my recommendation on physiological reasoning (the acute surgical stress response and anaesthesia effects have largely resolved, and the patient has not yet returned to full activity), but it is speculative. It should be validated by checking, in your own data, when the post-op RHR/temperature trajectory of uncomplicated patients flattens.
- Daily wearable aggregates are autocorrelated, and the PEB model assumes independent samples (the source paper requires >=24 h between samples specifically to avoid this). Autocorrelation means your effective n is smaller than your calendar n, so B_n as computed is optimistic. A defensible correction is n_eff = n*(1-rho)/(1+rho) using the lag-1 autocorrelation of the baseline residuals; whether that correction is well-calibrated for wearable series is an open question worth a simulation.
- Whether MedPull should ever run a full hierarchical Bayesian model is genuinely unclear at 10-1000 patients. The PEB closed form captures most of the benefit. The concrete decision rule I would use: run NumPyro only when you have >=100 patients AND a clinical question that requires calibrated uncertainty on a per-patient parameter (not just a point estimate). Otherwise the operational cost of a sampler in production is not repaid.
- How to combine the shrinkage framework with the eventual rare outcome labels is unresolved. With single-digit-percent event rates and hundreds of patients you will have single-digit to low-double-digit events, which is far below the ~10-events-per-parameter rule for even a logistic model. The realistic path is that outcomes validate and recalibrate thresholds (Youden or cost-weighted threshold selection on a small labelled set) rather than train a supervised model — worth deciding explicitly and early so data collection is designed for calibration rather than for training.

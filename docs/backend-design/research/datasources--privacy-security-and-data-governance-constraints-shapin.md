# Privacy, security, and data governance constraints shaping the Recovery Copilot backend (HIPAA, state health-privacy law, LLM BAAs, AWS PHI architecture, de-identification limits, RTM consent)

## Summary

Once wearable data is ingested on behalf of a treating orthopedic practice, Recovery Copilot is a HIPAA business associate and every Observation row is ePHI; the only lawful paths for that data are services under a BAA chain (AWS account-level BAA via Artifact covers the whole current stack; Lambda, S3, DynamoDB, RDS, API Gateway, CloudFront, Bedrock, SageMaker, Comprehend Medical, and HealthLake are all HIPAA-eligible). The January 2025 Security Rule NPRM (RIN 0945-AA22) would make encryption, MFA, asset inventories, network maps, semiannual vulnerability scans, and annual pen tests mandatory with a roughly 240-day compliance window, but as of July 2026 no final rule exists and OMB now targets July 2027 — build to it anyway since it is cheap now and expensive later. On the LLM question: sending PHI to any third-party inference API requires a BAA; Groq does publish a Business Associate Addendum (effective Oct 15, 2025) but it excludes free tiers, so the project's current free-tier Groq narrative path cannot legally carry PHI — either execute the paid-tier BAA or keep the LLM behind a strict de-identified-feature firewall. That firewall must be real: Na et al. (JAMA Network Open 2018) re-identified 94.9% of adults from 20-minute-aggregated NHANES accelerometer data plus demographics, so minute-level wearable series can essentially never satisfy Safe Harbor; pass relative post-op day indices and derived features, never calendar-dated time series. State law adds a second consent layer beyond HIPAA — Washington's My Health My Data Act carries a private right of action and demands separate opt-in consent for any collection/sharing beyond the requested service (HIPAA-regulated data is exempt, but consumer-facing app features may not be). For go-to-market, RTM billing requires documented patient consent at or before service start (CPT 98975/98977/98980-81), and practices will expect SOC 2 Type II at minimum, with HITRUST (e1/i1/r2 tiers) increasingly required by hospital systems.

## Findings

### 2025 HIPAA Security Rule NPRM: mandatory encryption/MFA, but final rule slipped to ~July 2027
*[strong]*

NPRM published in the Federal Register Jan 6, 2025 (RIN 0945-AA22); comment period closed Mar 7, 2025 with ~5,000 comments. It eliminates the required/addressable distinction and mandates: encryption of ePHI at rest and in transit, MFA for all systems accessing ePHI (limited exceptions), a technology asset inventory and network map reviewed at least every 12 months, vulnerability scanning at least every 6 months, penetration testing at least every 12 months, network segmentation, defined restoration timelines (72-hour restore of critical systems), annual compliance audits, and annual written verification/certification of business associates' safeguards. Timeline once finalized: effective 60 days after publication + 180-day compliance window (~240 days), BAAs updated within 1 year. HHS estimated first-year industry cost ~$9B. As of July 2026 NO final rule has issued; OMB Unified Agenda targets July 2027 for final action (pushed from spring 2026).

> Medcurity / Qubika / Elisity analyses of HHS NPRM, 2025-2026: https://medcurity.com/hipaa-security-rule-2026-update/ ; https://qubika.com/blog/hipaa-security-rule-2025-nprm-changes-preparation/ ; https://www.elisity.com/blog/hipaa-security-rule-2026-240-days

### Wearable data becomes PHI the moment it is held by us on behalf of a treating provider
*[strong]*

HIPAA attaches to data created, received, maintained, or transmitted by a covered entity or its business associate (45 CFR 160.103). Fitbit/Terra data held only by the consumer-facing vendor is not PHI; the identical stream becomes ePHI when Recovery Copilot ingests it to monitor a surgeon's post-op patients, because the platform then acts as a business associate of the practice. Every downstream vendor touching that data (wearable aggregator like Terra/Junction, AWS, any LLM inference provider) is a subcontractor business associate needing its own BAA (45 CFR 164.502(e), 164.314(a)). Non-HIPAA consumer paths instead fall under the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule (finalized amendments 2024; GoodRx $1.5M and BetterHelp $7.8M actions in 2023).

> 45 CFR 160.103, 164.502(e); FTC HBNR final rule 2024 (16 CFR Part 318); FTC v. GoodRx (2023)

### Groq DOES offer a BAA — Business Associate Addendum effective Oct 15, 2025 — but it excludes free tiers
*[strong]*

Groq publishes a Customer Business Associate Addendum at console.groq.com/docs/legal/customer-business-associate-addendum. Coverage: 'Covered Cloud Services' as listed in Groq's legal docs, explicitly EXCLUDING beta features, alpha/demo versions, and free tiers. Prerequisites: an executed Groq Services Agreement and the customer being a HIPAA covered entity or business associate. Terms: 'reasonably appropriate' administrative/technical/physical safeguards; breach reporting within 10 business days of discovery; individual access/amendment requests and accounting of disclosures processed within 10 business days; subcontractor flow-down required; unsuccessful security incidents (port scans, failed attacks) exempt from per-incident reporting. Consequence for this project: the current free-tier Groq narrative chain cannot carry PHI; PHI use requires paid covered services plus the executed addendum.

> Groq Business Associate Addendum, effective 2025-10-15: https://console.groq.com/docs/legal/customer-business-associate-addendum

### Anthropic BAA covers 1P API and Claude Enterprise, with dated feature carve-ins and 30-day retention on covered models
*[strong]*

Anthropic signs BAAs for the first-party API and Claude Enterprise; a single BAA can cover both if signed after Dec 2, 2025. Activation requires the org Primary Owner to sign the BAA and have Anthropic enable HIPAA compliance on the org. Covered Models require 30-day data retention and cannot run zero-data-retention; conversely some Claude Code features require ZDR ('qualified accounts only'). Excluded from BAA coverage: consumer plans (Free/Pro/Max), Workbench/Console, Batch API, Files API, Skills API, Code Execution, Computer Use, Web Fetch, and third-party MCP/connector data flows. Messages API features (prompt caching, structured outputs, memory, web search, tools) only covered for BAAs effective after Apr 1, 2026. Claude is also available under the hyperscalers' BAAs via AWS Bedrock, Google Vertex, and Azure.

> Anthropic Privacy Center, 'Business Associate Agreements (BAA) for Commercial Customers' (2025-2026): https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/8114513

### OpenAI: BAA for the API (application-based approval) and 'ChatGPT for Healthcare' (Jan 2026); no BAA for Free/Plus/Team/standard Enterprise
*[moderate]*

Per HIPAA Journal's 2026 analysis, OpenAI will not sign a BAA for ChatGPT Free, Plus, Team, or standard Enterprise. It offers BAAs for (1) the developer API, subject to an application/approval process (historically via baa@openai.com with zero-data-retention granted per-endpoint for approved use cases), and (2) ChatGPT for Healthcare, an enterprise product launched January 2026 where prompt PHI is excluded from training. Google Cloud's HIPAA BAA covers Vertex AI for Gemini and partner models.

### Re-identification: 20-minute aggregated accelerometry re-identifies ~95% of adults — wearable time series are a fingerprint
*[strong]*

Na, Yang, Lo, Zhao, Fukuoka & Aswani, JAMA Network Open, Dec 7, 2018: using NHANES 2003-04 (4,720 adults, 2,427 children) and 2005-06 (4,765 adults, 2,539 children), random forests matched demographics + 20-minute-aggregated physical-activity data to individual records with 94.9% accuracy (adults 2003-04), 93.8% (adults 2005-06), 87.4% and 85.5% (children); linear SVM achieved 84.8-85.6% (adults). The authors note raw accelerometer data at 100 Hz over ~2 years approaches human-genome scale, functioning as a unique fingerprint. Related work: de Montjoye et al. (Science 2015) re-identified 90% of people from 4 spatiotemporal points; gait patterns are biometric identifiers. Implication: stripping the 18 Safe Harbor identifiers from minute-level step/HR series does NOT de-identify it; only Expert Determination with aggressive aggregation/perturbation is defensible.

> Na L et al., JAMA Netw Open. 2018;1(8):e186040 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30646312/

### Safe Harbor's 18 identifiers make dated, geolocated time series categorically problematic
*[strong]*

45 CFR 164.514(b)(2) Safe Harbor requires removal of 18 identifier classes including: all geographic subdivisions smaller than a state except the first 3 ZIP digits (and only if the 3-digit ZIP zone has >20,000 people, else '000'); ALL elements of dates except year that relate to the individual (birth, admission, discharge, death, service dates — which kills calendar-stamped observation rows); all ages >89 aggregated to '90+'; device identifiers and serial numbers (#13 — wearable device IDs); IP addresses (#15); biometric identifiers (#16 — gait/HRV signatures arguably qualify); and catch-all #18, 'any other unique identifying number, characteristic, or code.' Plus the entity must have no actual knowledge the residual data could identify the person — untenable given finding above. Expert Determination (164.514(b)(1)) instead requires a person with appropriate statistical/scientific knowledge to document that re-identification risk is 'very small' (HHS guidance, Nov 26, 2012); determinations are typically time-limited and method-specific.

> 45 CFR 164.514(b); HHS OCR De-identification Guidance (2012): https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/de-identification/index.html

### Washington My Health My Data Act: opt-in consent architecture with a private right of action; first suit filed Feb 2025
*[strong]*

MHMDA (RCW 19.373; effective Mar 31, 2024, small businesses Jun 30, 2024) defines consumer health data as personal information 'reasonably capable of being linked' to health status — explicitly covering bodily functions, vital signs, and precise location near health facilities. Requires: separate opt-in consent for any collection/use beyond what is necessary for the consumer-requested service; a SEPARATE consent for sharing; a signed, annually-expiring 'valid authorization' for any sale; a consumer health data privacy policy; deletion rights extending to backups; and bans geofencing (within 2,000 ft) of health facilities. Enforcement: violations are per se violations of the WA Consumer Protection Act, giving BOTH AG enforcement and a private right of action where the first three CPA elements are presumed (plaintiff proves only injury + causation). First lawsuit filed against Amazon, Feb 2025. Critical carve-out: PHI regulated by HIPAA is exempt — so BA-held clinical data is out, but any direct-to-patient app features processed outside the BA relationship are in scope, and the law reaches non-WA companies handling data of consumers whose data is collected in WA.

> RCW 19.373; Orrick/WilmerHale alerts on first MHMDA lawsuit (Feb 2025): https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2025/02/First-Lawsuit-Filed-Under-Washingtons-My-Health-My-Data-Act ; IAPP overview: https://iapp.org/resources/article/washington-my-health-my-data-overview

### Nevada SB 370, California CMIA, and Texas: narrower but real obligations; CMIA can make an app a deemed 'provider of health care'
*[moderate]*

Nevada SB 370 (effective Mar 31, 2024): mirrors MHMDA's consent + written-authorization-for-sale structure but defines consumer health data narrowly (data the entity actually USES to identify health status), exempts HIPAA-regulated data, and has NO private right of action — AG-only enforcement. California: CMIA (Civil Code §56 et seq.) §56.06 deems any business offering software/hardware (including mobile apps) to consumers 'for the purpose of allowing those individuals to manage their [medical] information' a 'provider of health care' — bringing patient-facing recovery apps directly under CMIA; remedies include $1,000 nominal damages per violation via private action plus actual damages; CPRA treats health data as 'sensitive personal information' but exempts CMIA/HIPAA-covered data. Texas: HB 300 defines 'covered entity' as ANYONE possessing PHI (broader than HIPAA), requires privacy training within 90 days of hire and EHR record access within 15 business days; the Texas Data Privacy and Security Act (effective Jul 1, 2024) requires opt-in consent for processing sensitive data including health data.

> Bass Berry & Sims on NV SB370 (2024): https://www.bassberry.com/news/nevada-consumer-health-data-law-takes-effect-on-march-31-2024/ ; FPF WA/NV comparison chart: https://fpf.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/NV_WA-Comparison-Chart-FINAL.pdf ; Cal. Civ. Code §56.06; Tex. HSC §181 (HB 300)

### Information blocking: we are NOT an 'actor' while uncertified, but our provider customers are; developer/HIN CMPs reach $1M per violation
*[strong]*

ONC/ASTP confirms the 21st Century Cures information blocking rule (45 CFR Part 171) applies to three actor classes: healthcare providers, developers of CERTIFIED health IT, and HIEs/HINs. A non-certified app developer is not an actor — Recovery Copilot is out of direct scope unless it certifies a module or functions as an HIN. But its orthopedic-practice customers ARE actors under a 'knows the practice is unreasonable' standard, so the platform must not create contractual or technical barriers to their sharing of EHI (e.g., no exclusivity over patient-generated data, support export). OIG enforcement began Sept 1, 2023 with civil monetary penalties up to $1,000,000 per violation for developers/HINs; a separate 2024 final rule sets provider disincentives (e.g., MIPS/Medicare consequences); OIG issued an enforcement alert Sept 2025. Ten regulatory exceptions exist (preventing harm, privacy, security, infeasibility, manner, fees, licensing, etc.).

> ONC/ASTP HealthIT.gov information blocking topic page (accessed 2026): https://www.healthit.gov/topic/information-blocking ; 45 CFR Part 171; OIG CMP final rule (88 FR 42820, 2023)

### HIPAA operational floor: 30-day patient access, §164.312(b) audit controls, 6-year documentation retention, minimum necessary
*[strong]*

Right of access (45 CFR 164.524): individuals get copies of their designated record set within 30 days (one 30-day extension), in the form/format requested if readily producible, at a reasonable cost-based fee — OCR's Right of Access Initiative has produced 50+ enforcement actions since 2019, mostly against small providers. Audit controls (164.312(b)): must implement hardware/software/procedural mechanisms that record and examine activity in systems containing ePHI — there is no prescribed retention for the logs themselves, but HIPAA documentation (policies, risk analyses, BAAs) must be kept 6 years from creation or last effective date (164.316(b)(2)(i)); medical-record retention is governed by state law (typically 6-10 years, longer for minors). Minimum necessary (164.502(b)) applies to all uses/disclosures except treatment, individual access, and authorized disclosures — it forces role-based access scoping inside the app. Technical safeguards today (164.312): unique user identification (required), emergency access (required), automatic logoff (addressable), encryption at rest (addressable), person/entity authentication (required), transmission integrity + encryption (addressable) — the NPRM converts all 'addressable' items to mandatory.

> 45 CFR 164.524, 164.312, 164.316, 164.502(b); HHS OCR Right of Access Initiative announcements (2019-2025)

### AWS: entire current and planned stack is HIPAA-eligible; BAA is self-serve and account-wide via AWS Artifact
*[strong]*

AWS's HIPAA Eligible Services Reference (list dated Jul 22, 2026; 200+ services) includes: Lambda, S3, DynamoDB, RDS (SQL Server, MySQL, Oracle, PostgreSQL, Db2, MariaDB engines only), Aurora, API Gateway, CloudFront (excluding Embedded Points of Presence), SQS, EventBridge, CloudWatch, Bedrock (with specific model exclusions noted on the current list), SageMaker (excluding Studio Lab, Ground Truth Plus, and Public/Vendor Workforce), Comprehend Medical, HealthLake, and Transcribe including HealthScribe. The AWS BAA is accepted self-service in AWS Artifact and covers the whole account across regions — no per-service BAAs. AWS's position: customers 'agree not to use these services for any purpose involving PHI without first entering into an AWS business associate agreement.' > AWS HIPAA Eligible Services Reference (2026-07-22): https://aws.amazon.com/compliance/hipaa-eligible-services-reference/

### SOC 2 Type II vs HITRUST for closing practice and hospital deals: ~$30-80k/3-12 months vs ~$20k-250k across e1/i1/r2 tiers
*[moderate]*

Typical 2025-2026 startup figures: SOC 2 Type II costs $15-40k in auditor fees plus $10-25k/yr for compliance automation (Vanta/Drata/Secureframe), with a 3-12-month observation window — total first-year ~$30-80k; this satisfies most independent orthopedic practices and MSK digital-health procurement. HITRUST now has three tiers: e1 (Essentials, 44 controls, 1-year cert) ~$20-40k all-in and ~2-4 months; i1 (Implemented, ~182 controls, 1-year) ~$40-80k and 3-6 months; r2 (Risk-based, 250+ tailored controls, 2-year cycle with interim assessment) commonly $80-250k+ and 6-12+ months, plus MyCSF subscription (~$8-20k/yr) and validated-assessor fees. Hospital systems and payers increasingly mandate HITRUST (often accepting e1/i1 from early-stage vendors); Epic's Vendor Services program and large IDNs frequently ask for HITRUST or equivalent third-party attestation. Pragmatic sequence for an RTM startup: SOC 2 Type II first, HITRUST e1 when the first hospital-system deal demands it. ### RTM billing consent: documented consent at or before start of service; 2026 PFS adds sub-16-day codes; licensure follows patient location
*[moderate]*

CPT 98975 (initial setup + patient education, once per episode of care), 98976 (respiratory) / 98977 (musculoskeletal device supply, requires 16-30 days of data per 30-day period), 98980/98981 (treatment management, first 20 min + each additional 20 min, requiring at least one interactive communication). CMS requires practitioner to obtain patient consent in advance of or at the time RTM services are furnished, and to DOCUMENT that consent in the medical record; consent can be verbal but must be recorded. Effective Jan 1, 2026, CMS finalized new lower-threshold codes (2-15 days of data, mirroring the 2025-26 RPM changes) — the most significant RTM change since 2022 — enabling billing for short post-op episodes. RTM (unlike RPM) can be billed by physical/occupational therapists under general supervision. Licensure: the billing practitioner must generally be licensed in the state where the patient is located during monitoring; cross-state post-op monitoring requires PT Compact privileges (~40 member states) or per-state licensure; consent and licensure state must be captured at enrollment.

> CMS CY2026 Physician Fee Schedule final rule; Tenovi RTM CPT Codes 2026: https://www.tenovi.com/rtm-cpt-codes-2026/ ; UTHealth billing compliance RTM guidance: https://med.uth.edu/mshbc/digital-health-services/remote-therapeutic-monitoring-rtm/

### Wearable aggregator BAA class: Terra and Junction (fka Vital) act as subcontractor business associates
*[moderate]*

In the HIPAA chain for this product, the aggregator receives identifiable device data on behalf of a business associate (us), making it a subcontractor BA that must sign a BAA with us (45 CFR 164.308(b), 164.502(e)(1)(ii)). Junction (formerly Vital) markets itself as HIPAA-compliant with SOC 2 Type II and signs BAAs for healthcare customers; Terra advertises HIPAA/GDPR/SOC 2 alignment with BAAs on enterprise plans. Apple HealthKit is different: Apple is NOT a business associate (data flows device→our app under user authorization), but Apple's App Review Guidelines 3.3.8(b)/5.1.3 prohibit using HealthKit data for advertising or selling to data brokers and require a privacy policy; the HIPAA obligations attach only once our backend receives the data. These vendor claims were not independently re-verified this session — confirm each BAA and SOC 2 report during procurement.

> 45 CFR 164.502(e); Junction (tryvital.io) and Terra (tryterra.co) security/compliance pages; Apple App Store Review Guidelines §5.1.3 (HealthKit)


## Implications for backend

- LLM gating rule: PHI may only leave the backend toward an inference endpoint that sits under an executed BAA. Concretely: (a) keep the existing deterministic fallback as the no-BAA default; (b) if Groq stays in the chain, execute Groq's Business Associate Addendum (effective 2025-10-15) and move off the free tier, since the addendum excludes free/beta tiers; (c) alternatively route through AWS Bedrock (already inside the AWS account BAA — check current model exclusion list) to avoid a second vendor BAA entirely; (d) regardless, build a 'PHI firewall' serializer for LLM prompts that emits only derived features — risk tier, typed reason codes, z-scores/deltas, and RELATIVE post-op day numbers — never name, DOB, calendar dates, device serials, or raw minute-level series (calendar dates and device IDs are Safe Harbor identifiers; the time series itself is a fingerprint per Na 2018).
- Storage architecture: SQLite on Lambda is not a defensible ePHI store (no at-rest KMS encryption story, no durable audit trail, ephemeral). Before real patient data: migrate the Observation store to RDS Postgres or DynamoDB with KMS customer-managed keys, TLS 1.2+ enforced, VPC isolation for RDS, and confirm the AWS BAA is accepted in AWS Artifact on account 099660945689. Keep PHI out of CloudFront URLs/cache keys, Lambda environment variables, and CloudWatch log lines (log by opaque patient UUID; scrub payloads in the webhook logger).
- Audit and access schema now, not later: add an append-only audit_log table (actor_id, patient_id, action, resource, timestamp, source IP) written on every read/write of Observations and analytics outputs — 45 CFR 164.312(b) requires it today and the NPRM hardens it; retain HIPAA documentation (risk analyses, BAAs, policies) 6 years per 164.316(b)(2)(i). Implement a patient-export endpoint (JSON/FHIR-shaped designated record set) to satisfy the 30-day §164.524 access right and to keep provider customers clear of information-blocking exposure — and never put exclusivity over patient-generated data in customer contracts.
- Consent as a first-class table, not a boolean: per-patient, versioned consent records with (purpose, scope, granted_at, revoked_at, capture_method, state_of_residence). Minimum rows per patient: RTM billing consent (documented at/before setup per CMS, backs CPT 98975), HIPAA-relevant sharing scope, and a separate MHMDA-style opt-in for ANY secondary use (analytics beyond care, model improvement) — Washington's private right of action plus per-se CPA violation makes secondary use without separate consent the single biggest legal tail risk for a wearable product. Capture patient location/state at enrollment to drive licensure checks (PT Compact) and state-law branches (WA/NV/CA CMIA/TX).
- Deletion and retention machinery: implement hard-delete workflows that reach replicas and backups (MHMDA deletion right extends to archives), a per-tenant retention policy config (state medical-record laws, typically 6-10 years, govern clinical data; do not silently keep raw wearable streams forever), and dedupe-key design that survives deletion (tombstones) so a deleted patient's data is not re-ingested by a replayed webhook.
- Role-based minimum necessary: scope clinician console reads to the practice's own patients and to the fields a role needs (164.502(b)); require unique user IDs and MFA on the console now — MFA, asset inventory, network map, 6-month vulnerability scans, and annual pen tests are all in the NPRM and cheap to adopt pre-scale; treat 'addressable' encryption as mandatory today.
- Never ship a 'de-identified data' product tier based on Safe Harbor for wearable series: with 94.9% re-identification from 20-minute aggregates, only Expert Determination over heavily aggregated exports (e.g., weekly cohort-level statistics) is defensible; budget for a statistician's determination letter if research/benchmarking exports are ever sold.
- Compliance-artifact sequencing for sales: AWS BAA + signed vendor BAAs (aggregator, LLM) + HIPAA risk analysis are the day-one blockers for the first practice contract; SOC 2 Type II (start observation window ~6 months before first enterprise security review, ~$30-80k first year) unlocks most orthopedic groups; defer HITRUST until a hospital system demands it, then start with e1.

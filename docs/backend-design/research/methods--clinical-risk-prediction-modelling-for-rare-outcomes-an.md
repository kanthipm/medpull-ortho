# Clinical risk prediction modelling for rare outcomes and small samples — what MedPull Recovery Copilot may honestly build and claim at 10–1,000 patients

## Summary

At your data scale you cannot honestly develop a multivariable clinical prediction model for infection/readmission/MUA, and you should stop describing the engine as risk prediction. Riley's sample-size criteria, computed for your exact outcome profile (5% event rate, C=0.75, 6 predictor parameters), require n=1,288 patients / ~64 events just to hold expected overfitting to 10% shrinkage; at a 3% event rate it is n=2,121. Even a 3-parameter model needs 661 patients. The old "10 events per variable" rule is not conservative-but-safe — it is simply the wrong functional form; Riley's formulas produce anywhere from 4.8 to 23 EPP depending on prevalence and expected R²_CS, and for your parameters they land at ~11 EPP, i.e. 10 EPV would already be an under-estimate. Penalization does not rescue you: Riley 2021 (PMID 33307188) showed ridge/lasso/elastic-net tuning parameters are estimated with large uncertainty precisely when effective sample size is small and R²_CS is low, causing considerable miscalibration — "they perform worse when most needed." Gradient boosting is worse still: the largest head-to-head systematic review (Christodoulou 2019, 71 studies, median n=1,250, median EPV=8) found a difference in logit(AUC) between ML and logistic regression of exactly 0.00 (95% CI −0.18 to 0.18) in low-risk-of-bias comparisons; the apparent ML advantage lived entirely in the biased studies. Do not use SMOTE: van den Goorbergh 2022 (JAMIA) showed imbalance corrections drove calibration intercepts from ~0.00 to −4.5 or lower at 1% event fraction with zero discrimination gain and negative net benefit above the 0.3 threshold — shift the decision threshold instead. Your honest 2026 path is: keep the deterministic engine as a *monitoring instrument* (no probability claims), run silent-mode prospective label capture with adjudicated outcomes, elicit the composite weights from clinicians explicitly, evaluate the existing tiers with sensitivity/specificity/alert-rate confidence intervals (81 events buys sensitivity ±0.10) and decision-curve net benefit, and only fit a penalized model — Firth or Bayesian logistic with weakly-informative priors on 2–4 parameters — once you have ≥100 adjudicated events.

## Findings

### Riley minimum sample size, computed for your exact outcome profile — the numbers are brutal
*[strong]*

Criterion (i), shrinkage S_VH≥0.9: n = p / ((S−1)·ln(1 − R²_CS_adj/S)). Criterion (ii), optimism δ=0.05: S = R²_CS_adj/(R²_CS_adj + δ·max R²_CS), then same formula. Criterion (iii/iv), precise overall risk: n = (1.96/MoE)²·φ(1−φ). max R²_CS = 1 − exp(2·[φ·lnφ + (1−φ)·ln(1−φ)]). I computed these numerically (Monte Carlo over a normal linear predictor calibrated to the target C-statistic and prevalence, 400k draws) for your case. φ=0.05, C=0.75, p=6 parameters: max R²_CS=0.328, anticipated R²_CS=0.041, σ_LP=0.98 → n_crit1=1,288, n_crit2=356, n_crit3=73 → **n=1,288, 64 events, 10.7 EPP**. Same but p=10 → n=2,148 (107 events). p=3 → n=661 (33 events). φ=0.03, p=6 → n=2,121 (64 events). C=0.80, p=6, φ=0.05 → n=840 (42 events). C=0.70, p=4 → n=1,400 (17.5 EPP). Note criterion (iii) with the default MoE=0.05 is trivially satisfied at low prevalence and is misleading — with φ=0.05 you should demand MoE≈0.01, which alone requires n=(1.96/0.01)²·0.05·0.95=1,825. So a defensible target for ANY 6-parameter model on a 5%-prevalence outcome is ~1,800–2,100 patients.

> Riley RD et al., Stat Med 2019 (PMC6519266, formulas verified verbatim) + Riley RD et al., BMJ 2020;368:m441; numbers above computed by me and reproducible from the stated formulas

### Events-per-variable rules are the wrong functional form, not merely a rough approximation
*[strong]*

Riley's worked examples show required EPP varying by ~5x with no change in methodology: the Chagas disease model (p=24, R²_CS_adj=0.288, prevalence 17.4%) needs n=668 → 116 events → **4.84 EPP**, i.e. 10 EPV would be wastefully conservative; the VTE recurrence model (p=25, R²_CS_adj=0.051, event rate 13.4%) needs n=4,286 → 577 events → **23 EPP**, i.e. 10 EPV would be a 2.3x under-estimate. The driver is anticipated R²_CS (a strong model needs less data) and outcome proportion. Your case (weak-signal wearable predictors, low R²_CS≈0.04) sits firmly in the 'EPV=10 is dangerously optimistic' regime. Also note 'p' counts *parameters*, not variables: a spline with 3 df, or a 4-level procedure factor, costs 3 parameters each.

> Riley et al. Stat Med 2019 (PMC6519266) worked examples

### Penalized regression is necessary but NOT sufficient at small N — the penalty itself is unstable
*[strong]*

Riley RD et al., J Clin Epidemiol 2021 (PMID 33307188) simulated uniform shrinkage (closed-form and bootstrap), ridge, lasso, and elastic net. Verbatim: 'penalization methods can be unreliable because tuning parameters are estimated with large uncertainty. This is of most concern when development data sets have a small effective sample size and the model's Cox-Snell R² is low. The problem can lead to considerable miscalibration of model predictions in new individuals... Penalization methods are not a carte blanche... They perform worse when most needed.' Practical consequence for you: if you fit elastic net at n=300 with 15 events, the cross-validated lambda will vary wildly across resamples and your calibration slope in new patients can land anywhere from 0.4 to 1.6. Shrinkage is still mandatory at small N (unpenalized MLE overfits and produces slope <1), but the right response is fewer parameters + informative priors, not a tuned penalty.

> PMID 33307188, J Clin Epidemiol 2021;132:88-96 (abstract fetched verbatim)

### Gradient-boosted trees do not beat logistic regression on tabular clinical data — the definitive head-to-head evidence
*[strong]*

Christodoulou E et al., J Clin Epidemiol 2019: systematic review of 71 of 927 screened studies; median sample size 1,250 (range 72–3,994,872); median EPV 8 (range 0.3–6,697). In the 145 comparisons at LOW risk of bias, the difference in logit(AUC) between LR and ML was **0.00 (95% CI −0.18 to 0.18)**. In the 137 HIGH-risk-of-bias comparisons, logit(AUC) was 0.34 higher for ML — i.e. the entire ML 'advantage' in the literature is an artifact of bias (unfair tuning of LR, validation leakage). Calibration was not assessed at all in 79% of studies; 68% had potentially biased validation. Conclusion verbatim: 'We found no evidence of superior performance of ML over LR.' XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost are inappropriate for you at N<1,000 with <100 events: they will interpolate noise, produce badly miscalibrated scores, and their apparent AUC advantage will not survive temporal validation.

> Christodoulou et al., J Clin Epidemiol 2019;110:12-22, PMID 30763612 (abstract fetched)

### SMOTE and other imbalance corrections actively destroy calibration and clinical utility
*[strong]*

van den Goorbergh R, van Smeden M, Timmerman D, Van Calster B, JAMIA 2022;29(9):1525-1534. Design: simulation (24 scenarios; event fractions 0.30/0.10/0.01; 3/6/12/24 predictors; training n=2,500 or 5,000; 100,000-row test set) plus an ovarian cancer case study (2,695 training, 518 events; 674 test, 140 events). Methods compared: standard and ridge logistic regression, with random undersampling, random oversampling, and SMOTE. Findings: calibration intercept in the case study moved from 0.06/0.05 (uncorrected, near-perfect) to **−1.32 to −1.50** after correction; in the 1%-event simulation from −0.05..0.03 to **≤ −4.5**. Calibration slope also degraded (RUS: 0.85 SLR / 0.93 ridge vs 0.99/1.03 uncorrected). Discrimination gain: none — 'the median AUROC of models trained on uncorrected data was never lower than the median AUROC of models after RUS, ROS, or SMOTE' (AUROC 0.79–0.80 throughout). Decision curves showed 'strongly reduced clinical utility', with negative net benefit at thresholds ≥0.3. Verbatim conclusion: 'Outcome imbalance is not a problem in itself, imbalance correction may even worsen model performance.' Recommendation: adjust the classification threshold instead of resampling; if you must resample, recalibration is mandatory.

> JAMIA 2022;29(9):1525-1534 (full text fetched)

### Class weights are a threshold shift in disguise — and they break the intercept
*[moderate]*

class_weight='balanced' in sklearn is equivalent to fitting on a re-weighted (pseudo-oversampled) sample; it leaves discrimination essentially unchanged and shifts the intercept, so predicted probabilities are no longer risks. If you use it, the intercept must be corrected back to the true prevalence: β0_corrected = β0 − ln(w1/w0), where w1/w0 is the ratio of positive to negative weights (the King & Zeng prior-correction / offset logic). The cleaner engineering answer for a monitoring product: fit unweighted, keep probabilities honest, and choose the alert threshold from the decision-curve/expected-cost argument. Every clinically meaningful sensitivity/specificity tradeoff you can get from reweighting, you can get from moving the threshold, without the calibration damage.

> Implied directly by van den Goorbergh 2022 ('similar results were obtained by shifting the probability threshold instead'); prior-correction formula from King & Zeng 2001, Political Analysis 9:137-163

### Calibration hierarchy: what you must report, in order of strictness
*[strong]*

Van Calster/Steyerberg framework: (1) **mean calibration / calibration-in-the-large** — mean predicted risk equals observed event rate; measured by the calibration intercept from logistic recalibration of the outcome on the linear predictor with slope fixed at 1; target 0. (2) **weak calibration** — calibration slope from regressing outcome on the LP; target 1.0; slope <1 means predictions too extreme (overfitting), >1 means too conservative (over-shrunk). (3) **moderate calibration** — flexible calibration curve, loess or restricted cubic spline (3–5 knots) of observed vs predicted, with a bootstrap or spline-based CI band; this is what a calibration plot should show, NOT 10 decile bins (deciles are noisy and threshold-dependent; Hosmer-Lemeshow is a p-value with no effect size and should not be reported). (4) **strong calibration** — unattainable in practice. Numeric summaries of the flexible curve: **ICI** = mean absolute difference between the loess-smoothed observed and predicted probability, weighted by the empirical density of predicted probabilities; **E50** = median absolute difference; **E90** = 90th percentile; **Emax** = maximum. Austin & Steyerberg (Stat Med 2019;38(21):4051-4065, PMID 31270850) introduced these and showed they discriminate calibration quality among logistic regression, random forests, and boosted trees for post-AMI mortality where visual inspection could not.

> Austin PC, Steyerberg EW, Stat Med 2019;38(21):4051-65, PMID 31270850; Van Calster et al., BMC Med 2019;17:230 (calibration hierarchy)

### Platt scaling vs isotonic regression at your sample size: isotonic is disqualified
*[moderate]*

Isotonic regression is non-parametric and needs roughly ≥1,000 calibration observations to avoid severe overfitting of the calibration map — it produces step functions with wide, unstable steps and can only output as many distinct probabilities as it has calibration bins; with <100 events it will overfit the calibration set badly. Platt scaling (fit a 1-parameter or 2-parameter logistic on the model's log-odds: p_cal = 1/(1+exp(a·f(x)+b))) is the correct choice under ~1,000 events, and the 1-parameter intercept-only variant (recalibration-in-the-large, offset the LP and refit intercept only) is the right choice under ~100 events because it spends only 1 degree of freedom. sklearn: CalibratedClassifierCV(estimator, method='sigmoid', cv=5, ensemble=True) — never method='isotonic' at your scale, and never calibrate on the training data (leakage produces a perfect-looking calibration plot).

> Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana ICML 2005 (isotonic needs large N; Platt better with <1000 examples); scikit-learn calibration user guide

### Deployment recalibration and calibration drift — and a CUSUM detector you already have the machinery for
*[moderate]*

Any model developed elsewhere (e.g. a published post-arthroplasty complication model) must be recalibrated to your population before use; the cheapest and most robust update is intercept-only recalibration (re-estimate β0 with the original LP as an offset), then slope recalibration if you have ≥100 events; full refitting requires the full development sample size. Calibration drifts over deployment time as case-mix, surgical technique, and device firmware change — Davis et al. (JAMIA 2017, AKI models) showed measurable calibration drift within 1–3 years, with flexible ML models drifting faster than logistic regression. Davis et al. subsequently proposed CUSUM-based calibration drift *detection* monitoring the running sum of (observed − predicted), which maps directly onto the CUSUM implementation already in your engine (k=0.5, h=5.0 in SD units of the calibration residual). Ship a monthly job that recomputes calibration intercept, slope, ICI/E90 on a rolling 6-month window and alerts on drift.

> Davis SE et al., JAMIA 2017;24(6):1052-1061 (calibration drift in AKI models); Davis SE et al., JAMIA 2020 (nonparametric updating to correct drift); recalibration hierarchy from Steyerberg, Clinical Prediction Models 2nd ed. ch.20

### AUC is insufficient for rare outcomes — the concrete arithmetic for your prevalence
*[strong]*

AUC is prevalence-independent and threshold-free, so it tells you nothing about how many false alarms a nurse will triage. Worked example at your realistic parameters: outcome prevalence 3%, an alert rule with sensitivity 0.70 and specificity 0.90 gives PPV = (0.03·0.70)/(0.03·0.70 + 0.97·0.10) = 0.021/0.118 = **17.8%**, i.e. **number needed to evaluate (NNE) = 1/PPV = 5.6 chart reviews per true event**, and an alert rate of 11.8 per 100 patients per evaluation period. Push specificity to 0.95 and PPV rises to 30.3% (NNE 3.3) at the cost of missing more events. Report the precision-recall curve, whose baseline (a useless model) equals the prevalence — AUPRC 0.15 at 3% prevalence is a 5x lift and is genuinely useful, while AUPRC 0.05 is near-worthless despite possibly having AUC 0.75. Always report: prevalence, AUPRC with the prevalence baseline drawn, sensitivity/specificity/PPV/NPV at the *deployed* threshold with 95% CIs, alerts per 100 patient-weeks, and NNE.

> Standard epidemiological identities; PR-baseline = prevalence (Saito & Rehmsmeier PLoS ONE 2015); NNE = 1/PPV

### Decision curve analysis is the correct primary utility metric, and there is a maintained Python package
*[strong]*

Net benefit at threshold p_t: NB = TP/n − (FP/n)·(p_t/(1−p_t)), compared against 'treat all' (NB = prevalence − (1−prevalence)·p_t/(1−p_t)) and 'treat none' (NB=0). The threshold p_t IS the elicited exchange rate: p_t = harm/(harm+benefit); a clinician willing to do 10 unnecessary telehealth reviews to catch one infection is stating p_t = 1/11 ≈ 9%. Python: **pip install dcurves** (MSKCC Epi-Bio, the Vickers group's own port), API: `dca(data=df, outcome='infection', modelnames=['tier_score','ewma_flag'], thresholds=np.arange(0, 0.36, 0.01))` then `plot_graphs(plot_df=df_dca, graph_type='net_benefit', y_limits=[-0.05, 0.2])`; also supports `harm=` and `prevalence=` for case-control-corrected prevalence, and `net_intervention_avoided`. DCA was the metric that exposed the SMOTE damage in van den Goorbergh 2022 (negative net benefit at p_t≥0.3) when AUROC showed nothing — that is exactly why you need it.

> pypi.org/project/dcurves (fetched); Vickers & Elkin, Med Decis Making 2006;26:565-74; decisioncurveanalysis.org

### Internal validation: enhanced bootstrap optimism correction, never a single train/test split
*[strong]*

At n<1,000 a hold-out split throws away the data you cannot afford to lose and gives a validation estimate with enormous variance. Use Harrell's enhanced bootstrap: B=200–500 resamples *with replacement at the PATIENT level*; in each bootstrap sample re-run the ENTIRE modelling pipeline (variable selection, spline knot placement, penalty tuning, threshold choice — omitting any of these is the single most common source of optimism-bias in the literature); compute apparent performance in the bootstrap sample and performance of that bootstrap model in the original sample; optimism = mean(difference); corrected = apparent − optimism. Report optimism-corrected C-statistic, calibration slope, calibration intercept, and Brier/ICI. The bootstrap-derived calibration slope doubles as the uniform shrinkage factor to apply to your coefficients (van Houwelingen–Le Cessie heuristic alternative: s = (model χ² − df)/model χ²), after which the intercept must be re-estimated. Steyerberg et al. (J Clin Epidemiol 2001) showed bootstrapping is materially superior to split-sample and to standard 10-fold CV for small samples.

> Steyerberg EW et al., J Clin Epidemiol 2001;54:774-781; Harrell, Regression Modeling Strategies 2nd ed ch.5; van Houwelingen & Le Cessie, Stat Med 1990

### External validation sample size — verified worked example shows why you cannot validate anything yet
*[strong]*

Riley RD, Debray TPA, Collins GS et al., 'Minimum sample size for external validation of a clinical prediction model with a binary outcome', Stat Med 2021 (PMID 34031906). Criteria: precise estimation of (a) the observed/expected ratio, (b) the calibration slope, (c) the C-statistic, and (d) net benefit at the intended risk threshold; you must pre-specify target SEs/CI widths, anticipated event proportion, anticipated (mis)calibration and variance of the linear predictor, and the decision threshold. Worked example (mechanical heart valve failure, event proportion 0.018): **9,835 participants / 177 events** for calibration and discrimination; **6,443 / 116 events** for net benefit at an 8% threshold. The **calibration slope criterion drove the requirement**, and the paper notes this 'will often be the case'. The widely-used rule of thumb of ≥100 events and ≥100 non-events (Vergouwe 2005; Collins 2016 suggests ≥200 events) is an absolute floor, not a target. Riley's 2022 time-to-event companion is PMID 34915593; Whittle/Ensor/Archer 2025 (PMID 40596983) extends this with closed-form solutions for models evaluated at a *classification threshold* — that 2025 paper is the one that matches your alert-tier use case and is worth reading in full.

> PMID 34031906 (abstract fetched); PMID 40596983 (Whittle et al. 2025)

### Validation hierarchy: internal-external cross-validation by site, then temporal, then true external
*[moderate]*

Once you have >1 clinic/surgeon/hospital, do NOT split randomly. Use internal-external cross-validation (IECV, Royston/Debray/Steyerberg): iteratively hold out one site, develop on the rest, validate on the held-out site, then meta-analyse the site-specific calibration slopes/intercepts/C-statistics with a random-effects model to get both a pooled estimate and a **prediction interval for performance at a new site** — that prediction interval is the honest number to quote to a prospective customer, and it is always much wider than the pooled estimate. Temporal validation (develop on patients enrolled before date T, validate after) is the minimum bar before any deployment claim and is the cheapest thing you can do as you accrue patients. True external validation = different investigators, different population, model coefficients frozen. Your engine's per-procedure logistic recovery curves f(d) are effectively site-agnostic priors right now; IECV by surgeon/site is exactly how you would discover that they are not.

> Debray TPA et al., J Clin Epidemiol 2015;68:279-89; Steyerberg & Harrell, J Clin Epidemiol 2016;69:245-7 ('Prediction models need appropriate internal, internal-external, and external validation')

### Reporting and risk-of-bias: TRIPOD+AI (2024, 27 items) and PROBAST+AI (34 signalling questions)
*[strong]*

TRIPOD+AI (BMJ 2024;385:e078378) replaces TRIPOD-2015 and is 27 items, deliberately method-agnostic — it covers regression and ML identically, so 'we used a rule-based engine' is in scope, not an exemption. It adds explicit items on fairness/subgroup performance, data provenance, human oversight, and open code/data. There is a TRIPOD+AI for Abstracts checklist and a TRIPOD-LLM extension (relevant to your narrative-generating LLM: it must be reported as a component and explicitly documented as not producing numbers). PROBAST+AI is the companion risk-of-bias tool: **16 signalling questions for model development quality/applicability and 18 for validation risk of bias (34 total)**, extending PROBAST 2019's four domains (participants, predictors, outcome, analysis) to AI-based models. Practical use: run PROBAST+AI on yourselves before a clinical partner or payer does. The most common fatal PROBAST flags for a startup are: outcome determined with knowledge of the predictors (your nurses see the alert before adjudicating the outcome — this is unblinded outcome ascertainment and it inflates everything), and inappropriate handling of missing data.

> tripod-statement.org (fetched); Collins GS et al., BMJ Open 2021 (PMID 34244270) protocol; PROBAST+AI structure per PMID 42151078

### Clustering: your effective sample size is far smaller than your row count
*[strong]*

Daily-resolution wearable data means 90–180 rows per patient, but those rows are highly autocorrelated within patient. With per-patient ICC ρ≈0.3 and m=90 days, the design effect is 1+(m−1)ρ = 1+89·0.3 = **27.7**, so 1,000 patient-days carry roughly the information of 36 independent observations. Every sample-size calculation above is in PATIENTS (or patient-episodes), not patient-days. Consequences: (a) bootstrap must resample patients, not rows; (b) any day-level model needs GEE with exchangeable working correlation (statsmodels GEE) or a mixed-effects logistic (statsmodels MixedLM / bambi) with a patient random intercept; (c) an alert-level analysis (patient-days flagged) and a patient-level analysis (ever-flagged) answer different questions and have different denominators — pick one and state it; (d) reporting 50,000 'observations' from 400 patients in any material is misleading and PROBAST-flaggable.

> Standard design-effect theory (Kish); Donner & Klar, Design and Analysis of Cluster Randomization Trials

### What to do with essentially zero outcome labels — the honest playbook
*[moderate]*

(1) **Reframe the product**: an unsupervised deviation-from-personal-baseline detector is a legitimate monitoring instrument and needs no labels; it just cannot be described with words like 'risk of infection', 'predicts', 'probability', or any AUC. (2) **Expert-elicited weights**: replace the arbitrary fixed weights (RHR .25, temp .25, HRV .20, RR .15, steps .15) with a documented elicitation — 8–12 orthopedic surgeons/PAs, SHELF roulette or a simple pairwise AHP, elicit both the weights AND the alert threshold as an exchange rate ('how many unnecessary reviews per caught infection is acceptable?'), record inter-rater spread, and publish the spread as your uncertainty. This converts an undefendable magic number into a defensible Delphi consensus. (3) **Proxy/surrogate labels available immediately**: unplanned clinic contact, opioid re-prescription, antibiotic prescription, PT session missed, PROM (KOOS/HOOS, PROMIS-10) drop exceeding MCID, ED visit. These are 5–20x more frequent than infection and are legitimate intermediate endpoints — but you must state that the model predicts the surrogate, not the hard outcome, and that surrogate-to-hard-outcome transfer is unvalidated. (4) **Active learning to prioritize adjudication**: when chart review costs money, use uncertainty sampling plus a hard-mined tail (label all flagged patients plus a random 10% of unflagged, so you can estimate the true FN rate and prevalence — pure uncertainty sampling gives a biased prevalence estimate and you will not be able to compute PPV honestly; the random arm is the fix, and its inverse-probability weights let you reconstruct population-level metrics). (5) **Silent mode**: run the engine with alerts stored but not surfaced, for a pre-registered fixed window, with an outcome adjudication protocol (2 blinded reviewers, kappa reported, disagreement adjudicated by a third) written BEFORE the window opens.

> SHELF elicitation framework (O'Hagan/Oakley); TRIPOD+AI item on outcome definition/blinding; Settles, Active Learning Literature Survey 2009; standard surrogate-endpoint caveats (Prentice criteria)

### What a startup with <1,000 patients may and may not honestly claim
*[moderate]*

MAY claim: 'detects statistically significant deviation from each patient's own pre-operative baseline'; 'flags X% of patient-days, median Y alerts per patient per month'; 'concordance with clinician judgment of Z% on N reviewed cases'; 'time-to-clinician-contact reduced by ...' (an operational, not predictive, claim); 'implements the published EWMA/CUSUM control-chart methodology of [citation]'. MAY NOT claim, at any N under ~1,000 patients with <100 adjudicated events: any AUC for infection/readmission/MUA; 'predicts complications'; 'X% sensitivity for infection' without a CI (with 20 events, an observed sensitivity of 70% has a 95% CI of roughly 46–88%); 'AI-powered risk prediction'; 'validated'; any comparison to a competitor's AUC. MAY NOT do at all: fit XGBoost/LightGBM on <100 events; use SMOTE; report Hosmer-Lemeshow as evidence of calibration; report performance from a random train/test split of patient-DAYS (leakage across days of the same patient inflates AUC by 0.05–0.20). Regulatory note: keeping every number deterministic, transparent, and clinician-reviewable (the LLM writing narrative only, never numbers) is what plausibly keeps you inside FDA's non-device Clinical Decision Support carve-out (21st Century Cures §520(o)(1)(E), criterion 4 — the clinician can independently review the basis); the moment you ship an opaque learned risk probability that drives action, that argument weakens considerably. Get regulatory counsel before that change, not after.

> Binomial CI arithmetic; FDA Clinical Decision Support Software guidance (Sept 2022); TRIPOD+AI reporting requirements


## Implications for backend

- Stop calling the tier output a risk prediction in code, API field names, and UI copy until you have adjudicated labels. Rename fields like `risk_tier` / `risk_score` to `deviation_tier` / `deviation_index`, and make the API response carry an explicit `evidence_basis: 'rule_based_unvalidated'` flag plus the typed reason codes. This is a one-day refactor now and a legal problem later. The LLM narrative layer must be constrained to never emit probability language ('likely', 'X% chance', 'high risk of infection') — add a lexical guard in the narrative post-processor with a unit test, since this is the exact boundary that keeps you inside the FDA non-device CDS carve-out.
- Build the outcome-label capture schema NOW, before any modelling work. You need: an `outcome_events` table (patient_id, event_type enum {ssi_superficial, ssi_deep, organ_space, readmission_90d, mua, ed_visit, unplanned_contact, antibiotic_rx, opioid_re_rx}, event_date, source enum {chart_review, ehr_feed, patient_report}, adjudicator_id, adjudication_date, confidence), an `adjudication_queue` table with a `sampling_arm` column ({flagged, random_control}) and `sampling_weight`, and an immutable `alerts_emitted` audit log with the exact score, threshold, and engine version at emission time. Without the frozen-at-emission score you cannot do a retrospective evaluation later; without the random_control arm you can never compute sensitivity or honest PPV.
- Every evaluation and resampling routine must group by patient, not by row. Add a shared helper (e.g. `app/engine/validation.py` with `patient_level_bootstrap(df, metric_fn, B=500, group_col='patient_id')` and `GroupKFold(groups=patient_id)` wrappers) and make it the only sanctioned path. With ~90 days per patient and ICC≈0.3 the design effect is ~28, so 1,000 patient-days ≈ 36 independent observations — any metric computed on shuffled patient-days will be inflated by 0.05–0.20 AUC through leakage. Add a lint/test that fails if any evaluation code calls train_test_split or KFold without groups.
- Ship a monitoring/calibration job before you ship a model. A monthly (or weekly) task that recomputes, on a rolling 6-month window: alert rate per 100 patient-weeks, tier distribution, per-metric coverage/missingness, and — once labels exist — calibration intercept, calibration slope, ICI/E50/E90, and a CUSUM of (observed − predicted) for drift detection with k=0.5, h=5.0 in residual-SD units. You already have EWMA/CUSUM primitives in the engine; reuse them on the calibration residual rather than writing a second implementation. Persist these to a `model_monitoring` table so drift is a queryable time series, not a notebook.
- Version and freeze the engine explicitly. Add a semantic `engine_version` to every emitted score and store the weight vector, thresholds, expected-recovery-curve parameters (floor, r, d50 per procedure), and confidence-gate rules as versioned configuration rows rather than constants in code. Any evaluation must be reproducible against the exact configuration that was live on the day the alert fired. This is a hard TRIPOD+AI requirement and the thing that makes a future retrospective analysis possible at all.
- Do not add XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost, scikit-learn ensemble models, or imbalanced-learn (SMOTE) to pyproject.toml. Positively: pin `scikit-learn>=1.4` (calibration, GroupKFold, PR curves), `statsmodels>=0.14` (GEE, Logit, lowess), `dcurves` (decision curve analysis), and `firthlogist` (rare-event logistic). Adding imbalanced-learn is the single clearest signal to a reviewing biostatistician that the team has not read the calibration literature.
- Replace the hard-coded composite weights (RHR .25, temp .25, HRV .20, RR .15, steps .15) with a versioned, documented, elicitation-sourced weight set, and store the elicitation provenance (who, when, method, inter-rater spread) alongside it. Also expose per-metric contributions in the API response so a clinician can independently review the basis of every alert — that transparency is both a PROBAST+AI mitigation and the FDA CDS criterion-4 argument.
- Make the decision threshold configurable per customer and derived from an elicited exchange rate, not a global constant. Store `threshold_high`, `threshold_elevated` per site with the elicited harm:benefit ratio that generated them, so that when you eventually run decision curve analysis you can report net benefit at each site's actual operating point rather than a threshold nobody chose deliberately.

## Recommended stack

- **Sample-size gating: decide before any modelling whether you are allowed to fit a model at all, and how many parameters you may spend** — Riley four-criterion minimum sample size for a binary outcome (criteria i–iv), computed for your prevalence, target C-statistic, and parameter count; recompute monthly against actual accrued events via `No maintained Python port of pmsampsize exists as of mid-2026 — implement the closed form directly (about 30 lines of numpy) or call R's pmsampsize (>=1.1.3) via rpy2>=3.5. Formulas: n = p/((S−1)·ln(1−R²_CS_adj/S)); S_opt = R²_CS_adj/(R²_CS_adj+0.05·maxR²_CS); n_risk = (1.96/MoE)²·φ(1−φ); maxR²_CS = 1−exp(2·[φlnφ+(1−φ)ln(1−φ)])`
  - params: S_VH = 0.9; δ (optimism) = 0.05; MoE for overall risk = 0.01 (NOT the default 0.05 — at φ=0.05 the default is vacuous); anticipated C-statistic 0.70–0.75 (be pessimistic; wearable-only signals rarely exceed this for hard outcomes); φ = your observed adjudicated event rate, not a literature rate
  - needs: Zero — it is a planning calculation. But it tells you that a 6-parameter model needs ~1,300–2,100 patients and a 3-parameter model needs ~660
  - why: It is the only defensible answer to 'can we build this yet', it is what a PROBAST+AI reviewer will check, and it converts an argument into a number. Computed for you: φ=0.05/C=0.75/p=6 → n=1,288 (64 events, 10.7 EPP); p=3 → n=661; φ=0.03/p=6 → n=2,121
- **The model you actually fit FIRST, once you have 40–100 adjudicated events: a 2–4 parameter risk score** — Firth's penalized-likelihood logistic regression (bias-reduced MLE; handles separation and small-sample bias in rare-event data), with pre-specified predictors — no stepwise selection, no automated feature selection via `firthlogist (pip install firthlogist) — sklearn-compatible FirthLogisticRegression; note the original repo (jzluo/firthlogist) is archived, successor is 'firthmodels'. Alternative: statsmodels>=0.14 Logit with a manual Jeffreys penalty, or R logistf via rpy2`
  - params: FirthLogisticRegression(max_iter=25, max_halfstep=25, max_stepsize=5, pl_max_iter=100, tol=1e-4, fit_intercept=True, skip_pvals=False, skip_ci=False, alpha=0.05, wald=False) — use penalized-likelihood profile CIs (wald=False, the default) because Wald CIs are badly wrong at small N. Predictors: cap at 3 (e.g. composite deviation index, days since surgery, procedure type) so p≤4 including intercept
  - needs: ≥40 events for a 2-parameter model with wide CIs stated as such; ≥100 events before quoting any performance number; ≥661 patients (33 events) per Riley criterion for a 3-parameter model at φ=0.05
  - why: Firth's correction is the standard fix for rare-event logistic bias and monotone separation, which you WILL hit (e.g. every infected patient had a temperature flag). It gives usable coefficients and honest profile-likelihood CIs where MLE gives infinities. Riley 2021 shows tuned ridge/lasso is unreliable at exactly your effective sample size, so a fixed-form penalty beats a cross-validated one
- **Small-sample model where you genuinely have prior clinical knowledge to inject (the honest way to use expert elicitation quantitatively)** — Bayesian logistic regression with weakly-informative priors on standardized predictors; report posterior medians and 95% credible intervals; optionally a hierarchical patient/site random intercept via `bambi>=0.13 (formula interface over pymc>=5.10) or pymc directly; arviz>=0.17 for diagnostics. For a quick drop-in, R's arm::bayesglm is the canonical reference implementation`
  - params: Gelman's default: Cauchy(0, 2.5) prior on coefficients of predictors standardized to mean 0 / SD 0.5, Cauchy(0, 10) on the intercept — equivalent to a soft constraint that no single predictor multiplies the odds by more than ~e^5. If you have elicited surgeon priors, replace with Normal(elicited_log_OR, elicited_SD). Sampling: 4 chains, 2000 tune / 2000 draws, target_accept=0.9; require R-hat<1.01 and ESS>400
  - needs: Usable from ~30 events, but you must report credible intervals prominently and never a point AUC alone. Do not skip the Riley calculation — a Bayesian model is not exempt from needing data, it just fails more gracefully
  - why: At 30–100 events, the prior is doing the shrinking that a cross-validated penalty cannot do reliably (Riley 2021), and it is transparent and auditable rather than an opaque tuned lambda. It also gives you honest interval width, which is the single most important thing to communicate at your scale
- **Calibrating any score (including your existing composite deviation index) into an honest probability** — Platt scaling (logistic recalibration) — 2-parameter (intercept + slope) with ≥100 events, or intercept-only recalibration-in-the-large with <100 events; nested cross-validation so the calibration map is never fit on data the score saw via `scikit-learn>=1.4: CalibratedClassifierCV(estimator, method='sigmoid', cv=5, ensemble=True); calibration_curve(y, p, n_bins=10, strategy='quantile') for a first look, but use a loess/spline curve for the reportable plot (statsmodels.nonparametric.lowess with frac=0.7, or patsy restricted cubic splines with 4 knots at the 5/35/65/95 percentiles)`
  - params: method='sigmoid' ONLY (isotonic requires ~1,000+ calibration points and will overfit); cv=5 with patient-level GroupKFold(groups=patient_id) — plain KFold leaks across days of the same patient; ensemble=True. Report calibration intercept (target 0), calibration slope (target 1.0), ICI, E50, E90
  - needs: Intercept-only recalibration: ~25–50 events. Slope+intercept: ~100 events. Isotonic: ≥1,000 events — you will not have this, do not use it
  - why: Van Calster's hierarchy: without moderate calibration your alert thresholds are meaningless and net benefit can be negative. Platt is 1–2 parameters, which is all your event count can afford
- **Evaluating whether an alert rule is clinically worth anything, and choosing the threshold** — Decision curve analysis (net benefit vs threshold probability), with the threshold range set by clinician-elicited harm:benefit exchange rates; report alongside PR curve, PPV/NNE, and alert rate via `dcurves (pip install dcurves) from MSKCC Epi-Bio; scikit-learn precision_recall_curve / average_precision_score for the PR side`
  - params: dca(data=df, outcome='adjudicated_event', modelnames=['composite_index','tier_high','tier_elevated'], thresholds=np.arange(0.01, 0.31, 0.005), harm={'composite_index': 0.0}); plot with plot_graphs(graph_type='net_benefit', y_limits=[-0.02, 0.06]). Bootstrap the net-benefit curve (500 patient-level resamples) and plot the CI band — at <100 events the band will be wide and that is the finding
  - needs: ≥50 events to see anything; ≥100 events before quoting a preferred threshold. Below that, run it and report only the shape and the width of the uncertainty band
  - why: DCA was the analysis that revealed SMOTE's damage (negative net benefit at p_t≥0.3) when AUROC showed nothing. It also forces you to state the exchange rate explicitly, which is the honest way to set your 'high >2.0 / elevated >1.2' thresholds instead of picking round numbers
- **Internal validation of anything you fit or tune (including the existing rule thresholds)** — Harrell's enhanced bootstrap optimism correction with the FULL pipeline inside the loop, resampled at the patient level via `numpy + sklearn.utils.resample; sklearn.model_selection.GroupKFold(groups=patient_id) for any CV; no dedicated package needed — write ~50 lines and unit-test it`
  - params: B=500 bootstrap resamples of PATIENTS (not rows); inside each: refit thresholds, refit weights, refit calibration; compute apparent C/slope/intercept/ICI in the bootstrap sample and in the original; optimism = mean difference; corrected = apparent − optimism. Report all four corrected metrics plus the corrected calibration slope as your shrinkage factor
  - needs: Any N — but the correction gets larger and the corrected estimate noisier as N falls. Below ~50 events, expect optimism in C of 0.05–0.15 and report the corrected number only
  - why: Steyerberg 2001 showed bootstrapping beats split-sample and 10-fold CV at small N, and at your scale a hold-out set is unaffordable. Tuning outside the loop is the #1 source of the inflated ML results Christodoulou 2019 identified
- **Site/surgeon generalizability once you have ≥2 clinics** — Internal-external cross-validation (leave-one-site-out) with random-effects meta-analysis of the site-level calibration slope, calibration intercept, and C-statistic, reporting the 95% PREDICTION interval for a new site via `sklearn LeaveOneGroupOut(groups=site_id) + statsmodels.stats.meta_analysis.combine_effects (DerSimonian-Laird or REML), or metafor via rpy2 for the prediction interval`
  - params: LeaveOneGroupOut over site_id; per-site metrics with their SEs; random-effects pooling with REML; prediction interval = pooled ± t_{k−2}·sqrt(tau² + SE_pooled²). Requires ≥5 sites for a stable tau²; with 2–4 sites report the individual site results, not a pooled estimate
  - needs: ≥3 sites to attempt, ≥5 sites for a credible prediction interval; ≥20 events per site or the site-level estimates are noise
  - why: It is the only way to produce the number a hospital buyer actually needs ('what will this do at MY site'), and it uses every patient for both development and validation — critical when you cannot spare a hold-out
- **Zero-label period (now): getting to labels efficiently** — Prospective silent-mode collection with a pre-registered adjudication protocol + stratified active labelling (label 100% of flagged patients + a random 10% sample of unflagged, with inverse-probability weights) via `No modelling library needed. modAL>=0.4 if you later want uncertainty sampling; scikit-learn sample_weight for the IPW reconstruction; pulearn>=0.0.7 (Elkan-Noto) only if you end up with positive-only labels`
  - params: Pre-specify: outcome definitions (CDC/NHSN SSI criteria for infection; 90-day all-cause readmission; MUA within 6 months), the 90-day observation window per patient, two blinded adjudicators with Cohen's kappa reported and a third for disagreements, and the analysis plan — BEFORE the window opens. Sampling weights: w=1 for flagged, w=10 for the random unflagged arm
  - needs: Start immediately at any N. To reach the 100 events needed for a first honest model at a 4% composite event rate you need ~2,500 patient-episodes, or ~660 episodes if your composite outcome (any unplanned contact/antibiotic/readmission) runs at 15%
  - why: The random unflagged arm is non-negotiable: pure 'label what the model flagged' gives you no false-negative count, hence no sensitivity, no prevalence, and no honest PPV. This is the single highest-value engineering investment you can make in 2026 and it costs you a data model change, not a research team

## Open questions

- What is your real composite event rate? Everything above pivots on φ. If the honest hard-outcome rate is 3% you need ~2,100 patients for a 6-parameter model; if you define a composite operational outcome (any unplanned contact, antibiotic prescription, or readmission) running at 15%, the requirement drops to roughly 500–700 patients and becomes reachable in 2026. Deciding the primary outcome definition is the highest-leverage decision available and it is a clinical decision, not a statistical one.
- Who adjudicates outcomes, and are they blinded to the alert? If the same nurse sees the tier and then records the infection, outcome ascertainment is unblinded, every performance estimate is circular, and PROBAST+AI will rate the study high risk of bias in the outcome domain. Is a blinded chart-review workflow operationally feasible with your clinical partners?
- How many distinct sites/surgeons will you have by end of 2026? Internal-external cross-validation needs ≥3 sites to attempt and ≥5 for a credible new-site prediction interval. If the answer is 1, temporal validation is your only option and you should plan the cutoff date now.
- What is the intra-patient ICC of the composite deviation index in your existing data? This is computable today from unlabelled data (a variance-components model on the daily index) and determines your true effective sample size. If ICC is 0.5 rather than 0.3, your effective N halves again.
- What are clinicians' actual harm:benefit exchange rates? Are they willing to review 5, 10, or 30 patients to catch one infection? This single elicited number sets the decision threshold, the DCA threshold range, and the target PPV, and it should be measured before any threshold tuning.
- Is there a published, externally validated post-arthroplasty complication model whose coefficients you could recalibrate to your population rather than developing your own? Intercept-only recalibration needs ~25–50 events, versus ~1,300 patients to develop from scratch — this is potentially a 20x reduction in the data required to ship something defensible, and it should be investigated before any in-house development.
- How well do the per-procedure logistic recovery curves f(d) = floor + (1−floor)/(1+exp(−r(d−d50))) actually fit your observed steps/walking-speed data, and where did floor, r, and d50 come from? If they were hand-set rather than fitted, they are themselves unvalidated parameters that a reviewer will count against the model's parameter budget.
- Does the wearable data pipeline have systematic missingness correlated with the outcome (sicker patients wear the device less, or charge it less)? Informative missingness would bias both the coverage-based confidence gate and any future model, and it is testable today by comparing device-wear time in patients who later had unplanned contact versus those who did not.

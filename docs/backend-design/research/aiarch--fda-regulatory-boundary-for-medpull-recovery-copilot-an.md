# FDA regulatory boundary for MedPull Recovery Copilot analytics: non-device CDS under FD&C Act 520(o)(1)(E), the January 2026 CDS guidance (superseding September 2022), fallback pathways (510(k)/De Novo/PCCP), FTC and state AI-in-healthcare law

## Summary

The single most important finding: the September 2022 CDS guidance the task assumed is NO LONGER CURRENT. FDA reissued "Clinical Decision Support Software" on January 29, 2026 (superseding a January 6, 2026 version, which replaced the September 2022 final — verified by downloading the live PDF from fda.gov/media/109618/download today), and the new version is materially more permissive on exactly our crux: patient-specific risk predictions (e.g., 90-day post-operative mortality/complication risk for planning) are now expressly NON-device examples, provided the output is non-time-critical, reviewed by a clinician, and the full basis is displayed; near-term predictions (next 24 hours), real-time escalation, and alarms that "detect a life-threatening condition, such as stroke or sepsis, and generate an alarm or an alert" remain devices. Our current design — deterministic rule-based risk tier with fully displayed typed reason codes, episodic patient check-in data (not streaming signals), clinician-only worklist, LLM used only as a renderer of deterministic numbers — sits comfortably on the non-device side if we add a formal "basis panel" (inputs used, missing-data flags, plain-language algorithm description, validation summary) and keep all urgency language out of the product. The bright lines that would make us a device: outputting a probability that a patient HAS an infection, ingesting continuous/hourly wearable streams into the analytics, time-critical alarms, and any recommendation delivered to the patient rather than the clinician (patient-facing CDS is categorically a device). If we ever cross the line deliberately, the path is a 510(k) under 21 CFR 870.2210 (product code QNL — predicate: AgileMD's eCARTv5 deterioration suite, K233253, cleared June 2024) or SAK/880.6316 for sepsis-flavored prediction (Prenosis Sepsis ImmunoScore De Novo DEN230036, April 2024; Bayesian Health K250680, April 2026), at a FY2026 user fee of $26,067 ($6,517 small business) and a realistic all-in cost of $150k–$500k over 9–18 months, with a Predetermined Change Control Plan (final guidance Dec 4, 2024, revised Aug 18, 2025) to pre-authorize model retraining. Pre-Cert is dead (pilot completed September 2022; FDA says a real program needs legislation). The general wellness policy does not save us because our claims reference surgical recovery and complications. Separately, California AB 3030 (Health & Safety Code §1339.75) requires a GenAI disclaimer on LLM-written patient communications UNLESS a licensed clinician reads and reviews them first — which should be our design anyway, because it simultaneously keeps us outside the device definition.

## Findings

### The September 2022 CDS guidance is superseded — the operative document is the January 29, 2026 revision
*[strong]*

The PDF served today at fda.gov/media/109618/download is titled 'Clinical Decision Support Software — Guidance for Industry and FDA Staff', states 'Document issued on January 29, 2026' and 'This document supersedes "Clinical Decision Support Software" issued on January 6, 2026' (docket FDA-2017-D-6569, document GUI01400062). The Jan 6 → Jan 29 double-issuance in three weeks indicates a rapid correction cycle. The 2026 version restores concepts the 2022 final had stripped (the IMDRF risk-categorization reference, enforcement discretion for single-recommendation outputs) and adds explicitly non-device risk-prediction examples that the 2022 guidance would have treated as device 'specific outputs'. Any 2022-2025 legal analysis of criterion 3 ('risk scores are automatic device territory') is now partially superseded and must be re-checked against this text.

> FDA, Clinical Decision Support Software, guidance issued Jan 29, 2026, https://www.fda.gov/media/109618/download (fetched 2026-07-31)

### The four statutory criteria, verbatim
*[strong]*

Per section 520(o)(1)(E) of the FD&C Act (21 U.S.C. 360j(o)(1)(E)) as quoted in the guidance, a software function is not a device only if it meets ALL FOUR: (1) 'not intended to acquire, process, or analyze a medical image or a signal from an in vitro diagnostic device or a pattern or signal from a signal acquisition system'; (2) 'intended for the purpose of displaying, analyzing, or printing medical information about a patient or other medical information (such as peer-reviewed clinical studies and clinical practice guidelines)'; (3) 'intended for the purpose of supporting or providing recommendations to a health care professional about prevention, diagnosis, or treatment of a disease or condition'; and (4) 'intended for the purpose of enabling such health care professional to independently review the basis for such recommendations that such software presents so that it is not the intent that such health care professional rely primarily on any of such recommendations to make a clinical diagnosis or treatment decision regarding an individual patient.' Section 520(o)(3) reserves FDA authority to pull an exempt function back into regulation by final order if it 'would be reasonably likely to have serious adverse health consequences.'

> 21 U.S.C. 360j(o)(1)(E), (o)(3), https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title21-section360j; FDA CDS guidance (Jan 29, 2026) at Section II

### Criterion 1: our episodic check-in data is 'medical information', not a signal/pattern — but wearable streams would flip it
*[strong]*

The guidance: 'discrete, episodic, or intermittent point-in-time physiological measurements (e.g., routine vital signs obtained at discrete clinical encounters) generally do not, by themselves, constitute a pattern.' A 'pattern' is 'multiple, sequential, or repeated measurements of a signal or from a signal acquisition system.' Device examples make the line concrete: hourly pulse-oximetry and heart-rate measurements analyzed to identify deterioration = pattern = device (Example 24); CGM readings every 30 minutes = device (Example 25); even five sequential RR intervals from Holter data = pattern = device (Examples 20-21). Daily patient-reported pain/function scores and occasional discrete vitals typed into a check-in are Criterion-2 'medical information about a patient', so our current inputs pass Criterion 1. Ingesting continuous or high-frequency wearable data (step streams sampled continuously, HR time series) into the analytics engine would fail Criterion 1 outright, regardless of how the output is framed.

> FDA CDS guidance (Jan 29, 2026), Section IV(1) and Section V.C Examples 20, 21, 24, 25

### Criterion 3: recommendations and prioritized lists are fine; a 'specific preventive, diagnostic, or treatment output or directive' is a device
*[strong]*

FDA interprets Criterion 3 as software that '(a) Provides condition-, disease-, and/or patient-specific information and options to an HCP to enhance, inform and/or influence a health care decision; (b) Does not provide a specific preventive, diagnostic, or treatment output or directive; and (c) Is not intended to replace or direct the HCP's judgment.' Acceptable outputs: 'List of preventive, diagnostic or treatment options; Prioritized list...; or List of follow-up or next-step options for consideration (e.g., after a physician office visit, hospitalization, procedure).' New in 2026: where 'only one option is clinically appropriate and the software function otherwise meets all criteria under section 520(o)(1)(E), FDA intends to exercise enforcement discretion.' A non-device example directly on point for us: software that 'classifies patients with chronic low back pain into a single recommended appropriate clinical care pathway (e.g., conservative management or referral to a surgical spine specialist)' — but the same function for ACUTE back pain 'where immediate clinical intervention may be required' is a device.

> FDA CDS guidance (Jan 29, 2026), Section IV(3) and Section V.C introduction

### Risk prediction is now expressly non-device when non-time-critical and reviewed — the 2026 guidance's biggest change for us
*[strong]*

Non-device examples now include: 'A software function that predicts risk of future cardiovascular events for an HCP to consider' (device only if it 'predicts risk of a cardiovascular event in the next 24 hours'), and — nearly our exact fact pattern — 'A software function that estimates 90-day and 1-year postoperative mortality and complication risk following lung transplantation based on patient-specific clinical characteristics and published clinical evidence, where the output is intended to support pre-transplant planning and shared decision-making and is reviewed by an HCP.' The paired device counter-examples: predicting 'intraoperative or in-hospital mortality based on near real-time physiologic measurements... intended to guide immediate escalation of care', and models using inputs 'not well-understood and accepted for the stated clinical use.' So a post-op complication-risk stratification for routine clinician review can be non-device; a near-term, real-time, or escalation-driving prediction cannot.

> FDA CDS guidance (Jan 29, 2026), Section IV(3) example list (cardiovascular risk, lung-transplant risk examples)

### Criterion 4: the exact 'basis' disclosure recipe — and proof that inadequate disclosure alone makes an ML tool a device
*[strong]*

To satisfy Criterion 4, FDA recommends the software/labeling provide: (a) purpose/intended use, intended HCP user and patient population — and 'FDA does not consider software functions intended for a critical, time-sensitive task or decision to meet Criterion 4'; (b) required input medical information 'with plain language instructions on how the inputs should be obtained, their relevance, and data quality requirements'; (c) 'a plain language description of the underlying algorithm development and validation', including 'the general approach relied upon (e.g., meta-analysis of clinical studies, expert panel, statistical modeling, AI/ML techniques)', a description of the training data so the HCP 'can assess whether the data is representative of their patient population (e.g., relevant sub-groups, disease conditions, collection sites, sex, ethnicity)', and clinical validation results with limitations; (d) output showing 'relevant patient-specific information and other knowns/unknowns (e.g., missing, corrupted, or unexpected input data values).' Device Example 31 is decisive: an ML mammography follow-up tool trained on 100,000 cases, non-time-critical, HCP user — a device SOLELY because inputs weren't disclosed, dataset independence/demographics weren't available, and 'no additional information is available to the HCP to understand the key variables that influenced the recommendations.'

> FDA CDS guidance (Jan 29, 2026), Section IV(4) items (a)-(d); Section V.C Examples 31-32; Section V.B Example 3 (COPD steps/day tool) as the non-device template

### Alerts and alarms: the fatal framing is 'detect a life-threatening condition' + time-criticality, not the push notification itself
*[strong]*

Device Example 11: 'Software function that analyzes patient-specific medical information to detect a life-threatening condition, such as stroke or sepsis, and generate an alarm or an alert to notify an HCP... It does not meet Criterion 3 or 4 because it is intended to provide a specific diagnostic output or directive, including an alarm which supports time-critical decision-making.' Device Example 23: 'intended for HCP management of heart failure patients that analyzes patient-specific medical information (e.g., daily heart rate, SpO2, blood pressure, or other output from wearable product) to predict heart failure hospitalization' — device even at daily granularity, because hospitalization prediction is a specific diagnostic output supporting time-critical decisions. By contrast, non-device examples include notifications: drug-interaction 'notifications to avert adverse drug events', formulary-change alerts, and 'software function that flags patient results for an HCP based on specific clinical parameters... out of range test results where the reference ranges are predetermined' (Example 6b). The automation-bias discussion (Section IV(4)) adds that 'in situations that require urgent action, automation bias increases because there is not sufficient time for the user to adequately consider other information.' Translation: a non-urgent 'this patient's check-ins warrant review' worklist notification with displayed basis is defensible; an 'infection detected — act now' alarm is a device.

> FDA CDS guidance (Jan 29, 2026), Section V.C Examples 11, 22-25, 29; Section V.A Example 6b; Section IV(4) automation-bias discussion citing M.L. Cummings (2004)

### Patient-facing recommendations are categorically a device — the CDS exemption is HCP-only
*[strong]*

Verbatim: 'Software functions that support or provide recommendations to patients or caregivers – not HCPs – meet the definition of a device.' Criterion 3 is textually limited to recommendations 'to a health care professional' (statute: 520(o)(1)(E)(ii)), and the guidance's device Example 28 (patient-facing bolus insulin calculator) fails 'Criterion 3 because it is not intended for an HCP.' The HCP definition (footnote 1) is broad — includes NPs, RNs, PTs, pharmacists — so a practice's RTM nurse qualifies. What remains lawful patient-side without device status: displaying the patient's own entered data and trends (display functions under 520(o)(1)(C)-(D)/MDDS policy), general educational content, and messages a clinician reviewed and sent. What is device territory: any software-generated patient-directed assessment or recommendation ('your incision photos look concerning, contact your surgeon' generated autonomously), adaptive symptom triage that tells the patient what to do, or recovery 'status' framing that implies a clinical assessment.

> FDA CDS guidance (Jan 29, 2026), Section IV(3) (patients/caregivers paragraph), footnote 1 (HCP definition), Section V.C Example 28

### Where the LLM narrative sits: safe as a renderer of displayed deterministic outputs; a device the moment it performs its own clinical analysis
*[moderate]*

The guidance regulates functions by what they analyze and output, not by the technology; an LLM that verbalizes an already-displayed deterministic bundle ('worklist reason line', roster briefing) adds no new analysis and inherits the non-device posture — provided Criterion 4 basis (inputs, reason codes, limitations) stays visible and the text avoids 'specific diagnostic output or directive' phrasing. Three failure modes: (1) the LLM synthesizes new clinical judgments from raw check-in transcripts (e.g., inferring 'possible DVT symptoms' not produced by the deterministic engine) — that is software analyzing patient-specific information to produce a diagnostic output; (2) directive language ('start antibiotics', 'send to ED') — fails Criterion 3; (3) un-reviewable basis — a narrative whose content cannot be traced to displayed inputs fails Criterion 4 like device Example 31. The existing banned-language filter (blocking 'detect'/'diagnos' stems) maps directly onto the guidance's own device-trigger vocabulary ('detect', 'diagnose', 'identify [condition]'), and the mandatory 'monitoring signals for clinician review, not a diagnosis' sentence aligns with the intended-use disclosures in Criterion 4(a). LLM-drafted patient messages are fine only behind a mandatory clinician review-and-send gate (which also triggers the AB 3030 exemption).

> Analysis applying FDA CDS guidance (Jan 29, 2026) Sections IV(3), IV(4), V.C Examples 11 and 31; no FDA example addresses LLM narration directly

### Pre-Cert is dead: pilot completed September 2022, and FDA says a real program requires legislation
*[strong]*

FDA's own page: 'In September 2022, the Software Precertification (Pre-Cert) Pilot Program was completed with the issuance of the Report: The Software Precertification (Pre-Cert) Pilot Program: Tailored Total Product Lifecycle Approaches and Key Findings' (report dated September 26, 2022). 'Based on the observations from the pilot... FDA has found that rapidly evolving technologies... could benefit from a new regulatory paradigm, which would require a legislative change.' No successor program exists; the practical 'lifecycle' alternatives are the PCCP mechanism (below) and the Q-Submission pre-submission process for early FDA feedback.

> FDA, Digital Health Software Precertification (Pre-Cert) Pilot Program page (fetched 2026-07-31), https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/digital-health-software-precertification-pre-cert-pilot-program

### Cleared predicates exist for exactly our roadmap ambitions (deterioration prediction, sepsis, post-acute remote monitoring)
*[strong]*

Verified via openFDA: (1) eCARTv5 Clinical Deterioration Suite, AgileMD Inc., K233253, cleared 2024-06-21, product code QNL ('Medium-Term Adjunctive Predictive Cardiovascular Indicator', 21 CFR 870.2210, Class II) — EHR-based clinical deterioration prediction; QAQ ('Adjunctive Predictive Cardiovascular Indicator', same regulation) is the shorter-horizon sibling. (2) Sepsis ImmunoScore, Prenosis Inc., De Novo DEN230036 granted 2024-04-02, product code SAK ('Software Device To Aid In The Prediction Or Diagnosis Of Sepsis', 21 CFR 880.6316) — first FDA-authorized AI sepsis tool; (3) Bayesian Health Sepsis Flagging Device, K250680, cleared 2026-04-30, also SAK — proving SAK is now a workable 510(k) predicate path. (4) Post-acute remote monitoring platforms: Current Health System, K231506 (2023-08-24) and K222550 (2022-11-01), product code MSX (870.2300 physiological monitor network); Biofourmis Biovitals Analytics Engine, K183282 (2019-08-15), product code PLB ('Multivariate Vital Signs Index'); Visensia with Alert, OBS Medical, K110953 (2011), MHX (870.1025 arrhythmia detector/alarm) — the original multivariate early-warning index predicate. No 510(k) exists for 'mymobility'-style arthroplasty recovery apps (Zimmer's mymobility rides on wellness/MDDS positioning), which supports the view that PRO-based arthroplasty recovery tracking without deterioration claims is being marketed as non-device across the industry.

### PCCP final guidance (Dec 4, 2024; revised Aug 18, 2025) lets a cleared AI device retrain and update without new 510(k)s
*[strong]*

'Marketing Submission Recommendations for a Predetermined Change Control Plan for Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Device Software Functions' — 'Document issued on August 18, 2025. Document originally issued on December 4, 2024' (docket FDA-2022-D-2628). Statutory basis: FDORA §3308 added FD&C Act §515C. A PCCP authorized within a 510(k)/De Novo/PMA has three parts: Description of Modifications (which changes are pre-authorized — e.g., retraining on new data, performance improvements within stated bounds, input-source additions), a Modification Protocol covering (1) data management practices, (2) re-training practices, (3) performance evaluation, (4) update procedures, and an Impact Assessment. Appendix B's first worked example is 'Patient Monitoring Software' — i.e., FDA has already sketched the PCCP pattern for our device-side scenario. Practical upshot: if we ever file a 510(k) for a predictive tier, we should include a PCCP up front so quarterly model refreshes and threshold recalibrations don't each need a new submission (changes outside the PCCP still trigger 21 CFR 807.81(a)(3) analysis).

> FDA PCCP guidance PDF, https://www.fda.gov/media/166704/download (fetched 2026-07-31), GUI00020049; FD&C Act §515C (21 U.S.C. 360e-3)

### 510(k) realistic cost and timeline: FY2026 fee $26,067 ($6,517 small business); all-in $150k–$500k and 9–18 months
*[strong]*

FY2026 MDUFA rates (Federal Register, July 30, 2025, 90 FR 35893, Table 5): 510(k) $26,067 standard / $6,517 small business (gross receipts ≤$100M qualify; ≤$30M can get first-PMA waiver); De Novo classification request $173,782 / $43,446; annual establishment registration $11,423 (no small-business discount); 513(g) classification request $7,820 / $3,910. Non-fee spend (regulatory consultants, testing, quality system, validation study) typically $125k–$450k for a first software 510(k); De Novo adds clinical evidence burden and realistically 12–24 months. Budget planning number: ~$250k and ~12 months for a QNL-style 510(k) with eCARTv5 as predicate.

### The general wellness policy does not cover us, and 'enforcement discretion' is narrower than founders assume
*[moderate]*

FDA's 'General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices' guidance (2016, updated Sept 2019) exempts only (1) claims about sustaining general fitness/wellness with NO reference to diseases or conditions, or (2) healthy-lifestyle claims about reducing risk of certain chronic diseases where that association is well accepted. Post-operative orthopedic recovery monitoring inherently references a medical condition/procedure and complication risk — outside both prongs, so wellness positioning is unavailable. What actually triggers FDA attention in this space: marketing claims that outrun the exemption ('detects infection early', 'predicts readmission', 'reduces complications by X%'), adverse-event reports/press coverage (the Epic Sepsis Model controversy, JAMA Intern Med 2021, is the canonical near-miss that shaped the 2022 guidance's hostility), competitor complaints, and section 520(o)(3) final-order authority for exempt software 'reasonably likely to have serious adverse health consequences.' FDA polices the boundary primarily through intended use as evidenced by ALL marketing materials — website copy and sales decks count, not just the UI.

> FDA, General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices (guidance, referenced in CDS guidance fn. 14/20); 21 U.S.C. 360j(o)(3); Wong et al., JAMA Intern Med 2021 (Epic sepsis model external validation) — latter two from established knowledge

### State AI-in-healthcare laws 2024-2026: California AB 3030 binds our LLM patient messaging today; Colorado, Texas, Utah add disclosure/governance duties
*[strong]*

California AB 3030 (2024, Health & Safety Code §1339.75, effective Jan 1, 2025 — text verified): a health facility/clinic/physician's office using GenAI for 'written or verbal patient communications pertaining to patient clinical information' must include a prominent disclaimer (at the start of letters/emails; displayed throughout chat) plus 'clear instructions describing how a patient may contact a human health care provider' — but '(b) If a communication is generated by generative artificial intelligence and read and reviewed by a human licensed or certified health care provider, the requirements of subdivision (a) do not apply.' Our clinician-review-before-send gate is therefore a statutory safe harbor, and we should ship the disclaimer capability for practices that skip review. Also: California SB 1120 (2024) — utilization-review AI cannot deny medical necessity, physician must decide (applies to payers, not us, but signals the pattern). Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205, C.R.S. 6-1-1701 et seq.): 'high-risk' AI systems that are a substantial factor in consequential healthcare decisions impose developer duties on US (documentation, disclosure to deployers, impact-assessment support, algorithmic-discrimination care); effective date delayed from Feb 1, 2026 to June 30, 2026 (2025 special session). Texas TRAIGA (HB 149, signed June 2025, effective Jan 1, 2026): providers must disclose AI use in treatment to patients no later than time of service; prohibited-use list; AG enforcement with cure period; regulatory sandbox. Utah AI Policy Act (SB 149, 2024, effective May 1, 2024, as narrowed by SB 226/SB 332 in 2025): persons in regulated occupations (licensed clinicians) must prominently disclose GenAI interactions in high-risk contexts; Utah HB 452 (2025) separately regulates mental-health chatbots.

> CA AB 3030 text fetched from leginfo.legislature.ca.gov (2026-07-31); CO SB 24-205 as amended; TX HB 149 (2025); UT SB 149 (2024)/SB 226 (2025) — Colorado/Texas/Utah details from established knowledge (moderate for exact effective-date mechanics)

### FTC is the enforcement agency most likely to reach us before FDA: Health Breach Notification Rule + AI-claims substantiation
*[moderate]*

The amended Health Breach Notification Rule (16 CFR Part 318, final amendments 89 FR 47028, effective July 29, 2024) explicitly covers health apps and connected products not covered by HIPAA: a 'breach of security' includes any unauthorized disclosure — e.g., ad-tracker pixels leaking patient identifiers — not just hacking; penalties are civil penalties per violation per day. Precedents: GoodRx (Feb 2023, first-ever HBNR action, $1.5M civil penalty for sharing health data with Meta/Google ad platforms), Premom/Easy Healthcare (May 2023, $100k HBNR), BetterHelp (Mar 2023, $7.8M under FTC Act §5). On AI claims: 'Operation AI Comply' (announced Sept 25, 2024) targets deceptive AI capability claims; FTC requires competent and reliable scientific evidence for health-related claims — marketing 'AI that catches complications early' without validation data is a §5 problem even if FDA never calls. Note: if our practices are HIPAA covered entities and we sign BAAs, HIPAA (not HBNR) governs that data — but any direct-to-patient app features outside the BAA perimeter fall under HBNR.

> 16 CFR Part 318 as amended, 89 FR 47028 (May 30, 2024); FTC v. GoodRx (D. N.D. Cal. 2023); In re BetterHelp (FTC 2023); FTC Operation AI Comply (Sept 2024) — from established knowledge, high confidence in case names/amounts

### EU (brief): MDR Rule 11 makes almost any clinical software Class IIa+, and the AI Act stacks on top — do not drift into the EU casually
*[moderate]*

Under EU MDR 2017/745 Annex VIII Rule 11, 'software intended to provide information which is used to take decisions with diagnosis or therapeutic purposes' is Class IIa minimum (IIb if decisions may cause serious deterioration/surgery; III if death/irreversible) — there is no US-style CDS carve-out, so our clinician worklist tier would be a Class IIa medical device in the EU requiring a notified body, ISO 13485 QMS, clinical evaluation (MDCG 2020-1), and CE marking; software 'intended to monitor physiological processes' is likewise IIa+. The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) classifies AI that is a safety component of an MDR device requiring notified-body conformity assessment as high-risk (Art. 6(1)/Annex I), adding risk-management, data-governance, logging, human-oversight and transparency obligations, with high-risk obligations phasing in through Aug 2026–Aug 2027 (Annex I products effectively Aug 2027). Practical: EU entry is a separate, larger regulatory project than any US path; defer.

> EU MDR 2017/745, Annex VIII Rule 11; EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 6, Annex I, Annex III — from established knowledge

FDA maintains a public list of 'Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Medical Devices' (fda.gov/medical-devices/software-medical-device-samd/artificial-intelligence-enabled-medical-devices), exceeding roughly 1,200 authorizations by mid-2025, overwhelmingly 510(k) clearances (a minority De Novo, a handful PMA), concentrated in radiology (~75%+) and cardiology; deterioration/sepsis prediction entries (eCART, Sepsis ImmunoScore, Bayesian Health) confirm predictive monitoring is a normal Class II pathway, not exotic. ## Implications for backend

- Add a `basis` object to every analytics/LLM output contract (engine bundle → API → frontend): inputs consumed (field, value, timestamp), missing/stale-data flags, rule-trace for the tier (which typed reason codes fired and their thresholds), algorithm-description ID, and validation-doc version. This is the Criterion 4 artifact; it should be generated by the deterministic engine, cached with the same input hash, and rendered even when the LLM path falls back.
- The LLM must remain a renderer, never an analyzer: enforce at the contract level that narratives may only reference numbers/reason-codes present in the deterministic bundle (e.g., validate that every clinical assertion in LLM output maps to a bundle field; reject/fallback otherwise). Never let the LLM read raw transcripts to produce clinical flags — if transcript keyword triage is wanted, implement it as deterministic, displayed rules.
- Patient-message pipeline needs a hard review gate: a `reviewed_by`/`reviewed_at` requirement before send, immutable audit log, and a per-practice config that injects the AB 3030 disclaimer + human-contact block when review is disabled. Keep patient-visible strings out of the alert/detect vocabulary via the existing banned-language filter, extended per the do-not-do list.
- Notification design: worklist notifications must be batched/non-urgent ('N patients ready for review'), never per-event condition alarms; include 'not for emergency use' in notification templates and instruct patients (in check-in flows) to call the practice/911 for urgent symptoms — urgency must route to humans, not the engine.
- Data-ingestion boundary: schema-level rejection (or at least segregation) of continuous/high-frequency device streams; the risk engine may only consume episodic, patient-entered or discretely-recorded values. If wearable integrations ever land, they must feed a display-only surface, not the tier computation, pending a 510(k).
- Keep the deterministic engine's rules, thresholds, and golden-tier tests versioned and exportable — this is simultaneously the Criterion 4 plain-language basis, the Colorado impact-assessment input, and the design-history file seed for a future 510(k)+PCCP.

## Recommendation
**Stay deliberately on the non-device CDS side of section 520(o)(1)(E) — our current architecture already complies in substance — and harden it with a visible 'basis panel' and clinician-review gates rather than pursue any FDA submission now. Treat a 510(k) under 21 CFR 870.2210 (product code QNL, predicate eCARTv5 K233253) as a priced, planned option (~$250k, ~12 months) that we exercise only if/when predictive risk (probabilities, near-term deterioration, streaming-signal analytics) becomes the product we sell.**

The January 29, 2026 CDS guidance (which supersedes September 2022 and is more permissive than anything written about this space in 2022-2025) expressly blesses our exact pattern: non-time-critical, patient-specific risk stratification from episodic medical information, delivered to an HCP as prioritized follow-up options, with the full basis displayed for independent review. Every number our deterministic engine produces is explainable, which is precisely what Criterion 4 demands and what an ML tier would struggle to provide (device Example 31 shows an ML tool becoming a device solely through inadequate basis disclosure). The things that would make us a device — infection probabilities, 'detect sepsis' alarms, continuous wearable ingestion, patient-facing recommendations — are all things we do not currently do, and each has a cleared-device pathway with named predicates if we ever choose it deliberately. Meanwhile the binding legal obligations that apply TODAY are not FDA's: California AB 3030 (GenAI patient-communication disclaimers unless clinician-reviewed), FTC HBNR (no ad trackers on health data), FTC §5 (no unsubstantiated AI-accuracy marketing), and from Jan 1/June 30 2026 the Texas and Colorado AI acts. Those are cheap to satisfy now and expensive to retrofit.

**Do NOT:**
- Do NOT output a probability of infection, sepsis, VTE, or any specific condition ('72% risk of infection') — a likelihood of a present/near-term condition is a 'specific diagnostic output' (cf. device Examples 11, 29; Sepsis ImmunoScore needed De Novo). Keep the composite deviation index framed as deviation-from-expected-recovery, not condition likelihood.
- Do NOT ship real-time or same-day alarms framed as detection ('possible infection detected', 'deterioration alert') or anything marketed for urgent/emergent action — 'critical, time-sensitive' functions fail Criterion 4 categorically; alarm+detect+life-threatening is device Example 11 verbatim.
- Do NOT feed continuous or high-frequency device data (wearable HR streams, hourly SpO2, CGM) into the analytics engine — that fails Criterion 1 regardless of output framing (Examples 20-25); daily patient-entered check-ins are fine.
- Do NOT let any software-generated recommendation reach the patient without a named clinician reviewing and sending it — patient-facing recommendations are a device per se, and clinician review is also the AB 3030 safe harbor. This includes the roadmap's adaptive SMS check-ins: the bot may COLLECT structured answers adaptively, but must never advise ('rest and ice it', 'go to the ER') — route urgency to a human and static 'call 911 / call the practice' instructions.
- Do NOT swap the rule-based tier for an opaque ML/LLM-scored tier without simultaneously building the Criterion 4 disclosure package (training-data description, subgroup validation results, key-variable attribution); an LLM cannot currently explain its basis faithfully, so never let the LLM assign or alter the tier.
- Do NOT market with detection/diagnosis/outcome-improvement claims ('catches complications early', 'reduces readmissions 30%') — intended use is built from marketing materials, and unsubstantiated AI health claims are independent FTC §5 exposure.
- Do NOT put third-party ad/analytics trackers (Meta pixel, GA with identifiers) anywhere near patient-linked pages — post-2024 HBNR treats that as a reportable breach with civil penalties.
- Do NOT rely on the general wellness policy or on 'it's just software' arguments — our claims reference a condition (surgical recovery/complications), so wellness positioning is unavailable, and the only durable shelter is meeting all four 520(o)(1)(E) criteria.

# Time-series anomaly and change detection for individual physiological monitoring in post-operative orthopedic RTM (small-N, rare-label regime)

## Summary

The published wearable illness-detection literature converges on a result that should reshape the Recovery Copilot engine: the best-performing deployed algorithms are simple, nocturnal, single-signal, and personalized — not multivariate or learned. NightSignal (Alavi/Snyder, Nat Med 2022) achieved 80% sensitivity, 87.7% specificity and a 3-day median lead on 84 COVID cases using nothing but overnight resting HR versus a streaming per-person median with two thresholds (+3 bpm one night = yellow, +4 bpm two consecutive nights = red), and it beat both CuSum (72%) and the LOF-based RHRAD (69%) at matched false-positive rate. That is a direct, unfavorable benchmark for our current EWMA(0.3, L=2.66)/CUSUM(k=0.5, h=5.0) stack, which is a more complicated machine than the one that won. The two studies closest to our actual use case — Ghomrawi 2023 (162 children, post-appendectomy, Fitbit) and Hua 2025 (94 children, biorhythms) — used Balanced Random Forest with leave-one-subject-out CV and got AUROC 0.80/0.86 but AUPRC of only 0.36 and 0.14, with 387-438 false positives across a few hundred patient-weeks. That is the honest performance ceiling for our data scale, and it means alarm burden per patient-day, not AUC, is the metric we must design against. Our genuine differentiator over both papers is one they explicitly list as their top limitation: we have a pre-operative baseline, and they did not. Concretely we should keep the per-patient baseline, the expected-recovery-curve residual framing, the coverage gate and the typed reason codes; restrict vitals to a nocturnal window; replace the arbitrary fixed-weight composite thresholds (>2.0/>1.2) with per-patient conformal p-values that yield a stated false-alarm rate; run EWMA on Kalman local-level residuals rather than raw vitals so post-op drift stops latching; add BOCPD with a hazard of λ≈1/30 (not the paper's 1/250) as a regime detector; and add MEWMA with Ledoit-Wolf shrunk covariance as the principled replacement for hand-tuned weights. We should explicitly refuse CNNs, per-patient HMM/HSMM training, and matrix profile on daily-resolution data — all are inappropriate at 10-1000 patients with single-digit-percent event rates.

## Findings

### NightSignal is the single most transferable algorithm in the literature and it is simpler than what we already run
*[strong]*

Alavi et al., Nat Med 2022 (PMC8799466). Signal: overnight RHR only — mean HR over 24:00-07:00 restricted to minutes where step count == 0. Baseline: a STREAMING MEDIAN of average overnight RHR across all prior nights; the median stabilizes within ~7 nights for >80% of participants. Detector: a deterministic finite state machine with 6 states. Yellow alert: average overnight RHR >= baseline + 3 bpm for a single night. Red alert: two consecutive nights at >= baseline + 4 bpm (minimum 31-hour window). Reset to green when deviation falls below threshold. Cohort: 3,318 enrolled, 2,155 with wearable data, 84 confirmed COVID cases. Performance: sensitivity 80% (67/84 detected at or before symptom onset), specificity 87.7%, median lead time 3 days. Alarm burden: 3.42 alert-days per COVID-positive person vs 1.15 alert-days for non-COVID stress events. Head-to-head at matched false-positive rate it beat CuSum (72% sens) and RHRAD (69% sens). Chosen for being computationally trivial and portable across Fitbit and Apple Watch at differing data resolutions.

> Alavi A, et al. Real-time alerting system for COVID-19 and other stress events using wearable data. Nat Med. 2022;28:175-184. PMID 34845389, PMCID PMC8799466.

### RHRAD (Stanford/Snyder) exact production parameters, recovered from the authors' released code
*[strong]*

Mishra et al., Nat Biomed Eng 2020 (PMID 33208926); implementation at github.com/gireeshkbogu/AnomalyDetect. Online RHRAD: baseline_window = 744 hours (exactly 1 month) as training data, sliding_window = 1 hour for test data, outliers_fraction = 0.1 (this is the sklearn contamination parameter — the LOF/anomaly detector is told to expect 10% anomalous), random_seed = 10. Alerting rule is evaluated once daily at 21:00: RED if anomalies occurred continuously for more than 5 hours within the 24-hour period; YELLOW if anomalies occurred for one hour or continuously for fewer than 5 hours; GREEN if none. Offline variants: RHR-AD (resting heart rate) and HROS-AD (heart-rate-over-steps), both with outliers_fraction 0.1 and random_seed 10. Note the 10% contamination prior is an assumption, not an estimate — it directly sets the false-alarm floor at roughly 0.1 anomalous hours per hour monitored before the 5-hour aggregation rule suppresses it.

> Mishra T, et al. Pre-symptomatic detection of COVID-19 from smartwatch data. Nat Biomed Eng. 2020;4:1208-1220. PMID 33208926. Code: github.com/gireeshkbogu/AnomalyDetect

### Post-appendectomy complication detection from consumer wearables — the closest published analogue to our product, including its failure modes
*[strong]*

Ghomrawi et al., npj Digital Medicine 2023 (PMC10432429). N=162 children aged 3-17 (85 complicated appendicitis, 76 simple); 41 abnormal events in the complicated group, 10 in the simple group; 74 and 20 labelled 'abnormal' days respectively (defined as 1-2 days before reported symptoms). Device: Fitbit Inspire HR / Inspire 2; signals HR, physical activity, sleep. 75 engineered features including resting HR, HR mean/SD/min/max, steps, distance, calories, activity-intensity minutes, sleep minutes asleep/in-bed/restless, day-over-day changes, 3-DAY ROLLING AVERAGE DIFFERENCES, plus days-post-surgery and prior symptoms. Algorithms compared: AdaBoost, XGBoost, RUSBoost, balanced bagging, easy ensemble, Balanced Random Forest. WINNER: Balanced Random Forest (chosen for higher sensitivity). Validation: leave-one-subject-out CV. Performance complicated group: AUROC 0.80 (90% CI 0.76-0.83), sensitivity 83% within 2 days pre-event, specificity 74%, AUPRC 0.36. Simple group: AUROC 0.70 (0.60-0.80), sensitivity 70%, specificity 71%, AUPRC 0.03 — barely above a random classifier, and the model leaned on postoperative day rather than Fitbit data. Failure modes: 387 false positives (complicated) and 438 (simple); 7 and 3 missed events; NO PRE-OPERATIVE BASELINE available because appendectomy is emergent; single-centre; device generation changed mid-study with unverifiable proprietary algorithm drift.

> Ghomrawi HMK, et al. Applying machine learning to consumer wearable data for the early detection of complications after pediatric appendectomy. npj Digit Med. 2023;6:148. PMID 37587211, PMCID PMC10432429.

### Circadian/biorhythm features beat raw daily aggregates for post-surgical complication detection, and the AUPRC is brutally low
*[strong]*

Hua et al., Science Advances 2025 (PMC12239953). N=103 enrolled, 94 analyzed (ages 3-18); 13 children (13.8%) had postoperative complications — 10 intraabdominal abscesses, 2 surgical site infections, 1 small bowel obstruction. Device: Fitbit Inspire HR/2; per-minute HR and steps, daily sleep. 31 biorhythm metrics from three families: (a) circadian concepts, 7 metrics from activity — mean activity, relative amplitude, intradaily variability, episode counts and durations; (b) periodogram analysis, 5 metrics — power spectral density ratios and dominant rhythm periods; (c) COSINOR REGRESSION, 5 metrics per data stream — acrophase, amplitude, mesor, relative amplitude, model error. Model: Balanced Random Forest with NESTED leave-one-subject-out CV. Positive class = complication diagnosis day plus the 3 days prior. Performance: sensitivity 91%, specificity 74%, AUC-ROC 0.86 (95% CI 0.81-0.90), but AUC-PR only 0.14 (95% CI 0.08-0.22) and Matthews Correlation Coefficient 0.23. Lead time up to 3 days. Limitations stated: small number of complication patients, no preoperative baseline, and normal-recovery patients showing single-metric variance from individual differences.

> Hua R, et al. Biorhythms derived from consumer wearables predict postoperative complications in children. Sci Adv. 2025. PMID 40632861, PMCID PMC12239953.

### Fitbit's COVID early-detection algorithm: per-person z-scoring plus a CNN, and its sensitivity at usable specificity is poor
*[strong]*

Natarajan et al., npj Digital Medicine 2020 (PMC7705652). Signals: respiration rate during DEEP sleep (falling back to light sleep), resting HR during NON-REM sleep, and HRV as both RMSSD and Shannon entropy of nocturnal RR intervals. Baseline: per-individual rolling mean and SD producing z-scored equivalents — explicitly because of large inter-person variability. Model: convolutional neural network on 5-day windows of the z-scored metrics. Sample: 2,745 PCR-positive cases drawn from 187,573 survey respondents; 1,257 symptomatic individuals had sufficient physiological data. Performance: AUC 0.77 +/- 0.018; sensitivity 25.9% at 99% specificity, 43.7% at 95% specificity, 51.3% at 90% specificity. Detection at 95% specificity: 15% of cases on day D-1, 24% on D0, 33% on D+1. Respiration rate showed the largest and most prolonged deviation; HR rose during illness then fell after day +7; RMSSD and entropy both fell. Limitations: self-selected Fitbit users, unreliable self-recalled symptom dates, cannot distinguish COVID from other respiratory illness, and only 196 positives in the hospitalization sub-analysis.

> Natarajan A, Su HW, Heneghan C. Assessment of physiological signs associated with COVID-19 measured using wearable devices. npj Digit Med. 2020;3:156. PMID 33299095, PMCID PMC7705652.

### TemPredict/Oura: skin temperature adds real but modest signal, and the 'physiological disruption date' alignment trick solves a labelling problem we also have
*[strong]*

Mason et al., Sci Rep 2022 (PMC8891385). Oura Ring Gen2; five streams — dermal (skin) temperature, HR, HRV as RMSSD, respiratory rate, and METs. Model: an ensemble of five Random Forest classifiers on normalized physiological variables. Key methodological contribution: instead of aligning to diagnosis or symptom-onset dates, they aligned to a PHYSIOLOGICAL DISRUPTION DATE (PX) defined as the temporal midpoint between the maximal daily deviations in heart rate and respiratory rate computed over a 21-DAY WINDOW. Cohort: 63,153 participants, 704 self-reported COVID cases, training set n=73 PCR-confirmed, independent validation n=10 antibody-confirmed. Performance: AUC 0.819 (95% CI 0.809-0.830), sensitivity 82% / specificity 63% in training; sensitivity 90% / specificity 80% in the n=10 validation. Lead time ~2.75 days before participants sought testing; peak accuracy in the window from disruption to 2 days post-disruption. Including dermal temperature raised AUC by 4.9% (0.770 to 0.819). Failure modes: AUC 6.7% lower in women; AUC 0.730 for under-30s vs 0.900 for those in their 40s; participants with baseline dermal temperature above 38C were excluded; retrospective, unvaccinated, D614G-era only.

> Mason AE, et al. Detection of COVID-19 using multimodal data from a wearable device: results from the first TemPredict Study. Sci Rep. 2022;12:3463. PMID 35236896, PMCID PMC8891385.

### Scripps DETECT: sensors add ~0.09 AUC over symptoms alone — sensors are an adjunct, not a replacement, for asking the patient
*[strong]*

Quer et al., Nat Med 2021 (PMID 33122860). 30,529 participants enrolled 25 March - 7 June 2020; 3,811 reported symptoms; of these only 54 tested COVID-positive and 279 negative. Combined symptom + sensor model: AUC 0.80 (IQR 0.73-0.86). Symptoms alone: AUC 0.71 (IQR 0.63-0.79). Difference significant at P < 0.01. Signals were smartwatch/activity-tracker resting heart rate, sleep and activity. The practical lesson for us is the effect size: passive physiology bought roughly 0.09 AUC on top of self-report, on a cohort of 30k people that yielded only 54 positives. Our conversational check-ins are therefore not a secondary feature — they are the larger share of the discriminative signal, and the sensor stack should be fused with them rather than run standalone.

> Quer G, et al. Wearable sensor data and self-reported symptoms for COVID-19 detection. Nat Med. 2021;27:73-77. PMID 33122860.

### WHOOP respiratory-rate work: nocturnal RR gives real but late warning, and pre-symptomatic sensitivity is low
*[strong]*

Miller DJ, et al., PLoS ONE 2020;15(12):e0243693 (PMID 33301493). 271 individuals (age 37.3 +/- 9.5; 190 male, 81 female) contributing 2,672 person-days (1,856 healthy, 231 COVID-infected, 585 symptomatic-but-negative). Split: training n=57 COVID-positive (537 samples); validation n=24 COVID-positive (320 samples); negative validation n=190 (1,815 samples). Signal: respiratory rate during night-time sleep only, from the WHOOP wrist strap. The model estimated infection probability from CHANGES in nocturnal respiratory rate. Performance: identified 20% of COVID-positive individuals in the two days PRIOR to symptom onset, and 80% of positives by the third day of symptoms. The 585 symptomatic-but-negative days are the important control — this is the only study in the set that properly tests specificity against people who feel ill for other reasons, which is precisely the post-op patient's situation.

> Miller DJ, et al. Analyzing changes in respiratory rate to predict the risk of COVID-19 infection. PLoS ONE. 2020;15(12):e0243693. PMID 33301493.

### Bayesian Online Change Point Detection (Adams & MacKay) — exact recursion, and why their hazard rate is wrong for us
*[strong]*

arXiv:0710.3742. Maintains a posterior over RUN LENGTH r_t (time since last changepoint) by the message-passing recursion P(r_t | x_{1:t}) proportional to P(x_t | r_t, x_{1:t-1}) * sum over r_{t-1} of [ P(r_t | r_{t-1}) * P(r_{t-1} | x_{1:t-1}) ]. The changepoint prior is the hazard function H(r); the constant-hazard case H(r) = lambda yields a geometric run-length prior P(r_t = r) = (1-lambda)^r * lambda. For Gaussian data with unknown mean and variance the conjugate prior is Normal-Inverse-Gamma, N(mu | m, sigma^2/k) * IG(sigma^2 | a, b), giving a Student-t posterior predictive that can be updated recursively in closed form — no MCMC, no optimizer, O(t) per step (O(1) with run-length pruning). The authors used lambda = 1/250 uniformly across well-log, financial and coal-mine data without per-dataset tuning. CRITICAL: 1/250 encodes 'a regime change every 250 observations'. On daily-resolution 90-day orthopedic recovery we expect roughly one to three genuine physiological regime changes (surgery, inflammatory resolution, possibly a complication), so the correct hazard is lambda = 1/30 to 1/45, i.e. 0.022-0.033. Using 1/250 would make the model essentially unable to declare a changepoint within a single recovery episode.

> Adams RP, MacKay DJC. Bayesian Online Changepoint Detection. arXiv:0710.3742, 2007.

### PELT and offline segmentation via ruptures — usable, but only as a retrospective/analyst tool, not for daily alerting
*[strong]*

ruptures library (centre-borelli.github.io/ruptures-docs). API: rpt.Pelt(model=<cost>, min_size=3, jump=5).fit(signal).predict(pen=<penalty>). Cost models available: l1, l2 (mean shift, Gaussian), rbf (kernel, distribution change), normal (mean+variance), cosine, linear, clinear, rank (rank-based, robust), ml, ar (autoregressive). Average complexity O(C*K*n) for K changepoints, n samples, C the per-segment cost complexity. The penalty is linear in the number of changepoints; the standard BIC-flavoured choice is pen = 2 * sigma^2 * log(n) for the l2 cost with sigma the residual noise SD, or pen = p * log(n) * sigma^2 for p-dimensional signals. With n=90 daily points, min_size must be raised to at least 5-7 (a segment shorter than a week is not a clinical regime) and jump should be set to 1 since n is tiny and the speedup is irrelevant. PELT is exact and offline — it re-segments the whole series each time, so a changepoint can appear and then disappear as new data arrives. That is unacceptable behaviour for a clinician-facing alert but excellent for retrospective cohort analysis and for generating the labels/regimes we do not otherwise have.

> Killick R, Fearnhead P, Eckley IA. Optimal detection of changepoints with a linear computational cost. JASA 2012;107:1590-1598. Implementation: ruptures, centre-borelli.github.io/ruptures-docs/user-guide/detection/pelt/

### MEWMA is the principled replacement for our hand-tuned fixed-weight composite — but the covariance matrix is not estimable at our baseline length without shrinkage
*[strong]*

NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook 6.3.4.3. The MEWMA vector is Z_i = Lambda*X_i + (I - Lambda)*Z_{i-1} with Lambda a diagonal matrix of smoothing constants; the plotted statistic is T2_i = Z_i' * Sigma_Z^{-1} * Z_i. For equal smoothing lambda across all p variables the exact covariance is Sigma_Z = [lambda/(2-lambda)] * [1 - (1-lambda)^(2i)] * Sigma, with asymptotic form [lambda/(2-lambda)] * Sigma. NIST's worked example uses lambda = 0.10 with UCL 5.938 at alpha = 0.05. Standard ARL-optimal control limits (Lowry/Woodall/Rigdon tables) for in-control ARL=200: p=4, lambda=0.1 -> h4 = 12.73; p=4, lambda=0.2 -> h4 = 13.87; p=6, lambda=0.1 -> h4 = 15.26; p=6, lambda=0.2 -> h4 = 16.57. MEWMA dominates Hotelling T-squared for SMALL sustained shifts (below ~1.5 sigma), which is exactly the regime of early infection. THE BLOCKER AT OUR SCALE: estimating a 6x6 Sigma requires far more than the 14-21 pre-op baseline days we will have per patient — the sample covariance is rank-deficient or wildly unstable when n_days is not >> p. Fix: use sklearn.covariance.LedoitWolf shrinkage, or better, pool the correlation matrix across all patients of the same procedure and rescale by each patient's own per-metric SDs. Do not compute a per-patient 6x6 sample covariance from 14 days.

> NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods, section 6.3.4.3 Multivariate EWMA charts, itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmc/section3/pmc343.htm; Lowry CA, Woodall WH, Champ CW, Rigdon SE. A multivariate EWMA control chart. Technometrics 1992;34:46-53.

### Matrix Profile / STUMPY is the right tool for intraday data and the wrong tool for our daily aggregates
*[strong]*

STUMPY API: stumpy.stump(T_A, m, T_B=None, ignore_trivial=True, normalize=True, p=2.0, k=1) for the batch z-normalized matrix profile; stumpy.stumpi(T, m, egress=True, normalize=True, mp=None) is a streaming class exposing .update(t) plus attributes P_, I_, left_P_, left_I_ — the left matrix profile is the online-honest one because it only looks backward. stumpy.mstump(T, m, include=None, discords=False) gives the multi-dimensional matrix profile. Regime change: stumpy.fluss(I, L, n_regimes, excl_factor=5) returns the corrected arc curve and regime locations for static data; stumpy.floss(mp, T, m, L, excl_factor=5, n_iter=1000, n_samples=1000) is the streaming version with .update(t). WHY IT DOES NOT FIT OUR DAILY DATA: a meaningful window m needs to span at least one to two cycles of the pattern you are matching, and with only ~90 daily points a window of m=7 leaves 84 subsequences, of which the exclusion zone removes a large fraction — discord scores are then dominated by noise and are not statistically distinguishable. Matrix profile becomes appropriate the moment we ingest minute- or 5-minute-resolution intraday HR: at 1440 points/day, m=60 (one hour) or m=1440 (one day, for day-to-day motif matching) over a 14-day window gives 20,000+ subsequences and FLOSS regime detection becomes genuinely informative for circadian disruption. Deployment caveat: STUMPY depends on numba/llvmlite, which is large and slow to cold-start on AWS Lambda.

> STUMPY API reference, stumpy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/api.html; Yeh CCM et al. Matrix Profile I. ICDM 2016; Gharghabi S et al. FLOSS/FLUSS, ICDM 2017.

### Conformal anomaly detection gives the calibrated per-person-day false-alarm rate our fixed thresholds cannot, but small calibration sets impose a hard floor on achievable alpha
*[moderate]*

Split/inductive conformal anomaly detection: choose a nonconformity score A (our composite deviation index works directly), hold out a calibration set of n scores from days believed in-control, and for a new day compute the conformal p-value p = (#{i : A_i >= A_new} + 1) / (n + 1). Under exchangeability this p-value is valid — P(p <= alpha) <= alpha — with NO distributional assumption and no training labels, which is exactly right for us since we have no outcome labels. Use Mondrian (per-patient, or per-procedure-per-postop-week) conformal so validity holds within each stratum rather than only marginally. THE SMALL-DATA FLOOR: with n calibration days the smallest attainable p-value is 1/(n+1). With 20 in-control days the minimum p is 0.048 — you literally cannot assert a 1% false-alarm rate, and any threshold below 1/(n+1) fires never. To advertise alpha = 0.01 you need n >= 99 in-control patient-days in the stratum, which we get by pooling across patients, not within one. For sequential monitoring, wrap the p-values in a power martingale M_t = prod over i of (epsilon * p_i^(epsilon-1)) with epsilon = 0.92, and alarm when M_t > 100; Ville's inequality then bounds the false-alarm probability at 1/100 for the whole monitored trajectory. Exchangeability is violated by systematic post-op drift, which is why conformal must be applied to residuals from the expected-recovery curve, not to raw values.

> Laxhammar R, Falkman G. Inductive conformal anomaly detection for sequential detection of anomalous sub-trajectories. Ann Math Artif Intell 2015;74:67-94; Vovk V, Gammerman A, Shafer G. Algorithmic Learning in a Random World, 2nd ed. 2022; Fedorova V et al. Plug-in martingales for testing exchangeability on-line. ICML 2012.

### State-space / Kalman local-level models are the correct home for a 'personal baseline' that must legitimately move during recovery
*[strong]*

Our current design treats the pre-op baseline as a fixed constant with an SD floor, and then hacks around the resulting drift by truncating CUSUM to a 14-day window. The principled formulation is a local level model: y_t = mu_t + eps_t with eps_t ~ N(0, sigma_eps^2), and mu_t = mu_{t-1} + eta_t with eta_t ~ N(0, sigma_eta^2). The signal-to-noise ratio q = sigma_eta^2 / sigma_eps^2 controls how fast the personal baseline is allowed to adapt; the steady-state Kalman gain is K = (sqrt(q^2 + 4q) - q)/2, which is exactly an EWMA with lambda = K. So EWMA is the special case — but the state-space form additionally gives you (a) principled handling of MISSING DAYS, which our coverage gate says are common, (b) a time-varying prediction variance F_t so the control limits widen honestly when data is sparse, and (c) standardized one-step-ahead prediction errors (innovations) v_t / sqrt(F_t) that are i.i.d. N(0,1) under the null and are the correct input to CUSUM, EWMA, conformal or BOCPD. Implementation: statsmodels.tsa.statespace.structural.UnobservedComponents(y, level='local level') or 'local linear trend', with .fit() estimating sigma_eps and sigma_eta by MLE; for very short series fix q by hand (q = 0.01 to 0.05 gives a slow, credible baseline; q = 0.2 is far too fast for a physiological baseline). statsmodels handles NaN gaps natively via the Kalman filter, which is the single biggest practical win over pandas EWM.

> Durbin J, Koopman SJ. Time Series Analysis by State Space Methods, 2nd ed., OUP 2012; statsmodels.tsa.statespace.structural.UnobservedComponents documentation.

### STL and MSTL decomposition are only meaningful once we have intraday data; on daily aggregates there is no seasonality to remove
*[moderate]*

STL (Cleveland et al. 1990) decomposes y_t = trend + seasonal + remainder via iterated LOESS. statsmodels.tsa.seasonal.STL(endog, period, seasonal=7, trend=None, robust=False) — set robust=True to switch to bisquare weighting, which is essential for physiological data with outlier days, and seasonal must be an odd integer >= 7. statsmodels.tsa.seasonal.MSTL handles multiple periods, e.g. periods=(1440//5, 7*1440//5) for 5-minute data with daily and weekly cycles. AT OUR CURRENT DAILY RESOLUTION THERE IS NO USABLE SEASONALITY: a 7-day weekly cycle over a 90-day recovery gives ~12 cycles, which is marginal, and the true weekly effect in post-op patients (weekend PT sessions, clinic visits) is small relative to recovery trend. Recommendation: do not add STL now. Add MSTL with robust=True the moment intraday HR lands, at which point the daily circadian component is large, real, and clinically meaningful — loss of circadian amplitude is exactly what Hua et al. captured with cosinor and it drove their AUC 0.86. In the interim, the cheap version of the same idea is a cosinor regression on intraday HR: y(t) = M + A*cos(2*pi*t/24 + phi), fit as ordinary least squares on [1, cos(2*pi*t/24), sin(2*pi*t/24)] — three parameters, estimable from a single day of intraday data, yielding mesor M, amplitude A and acrophase phi as three interpretable daily features.

> Cleveland RB, Cleveland WS, McRae JE, Terpenning I. STL: A Seasonal-Trend Decomposition Procedure Based on Loess. J Official Statistics 1990;6:3-73; Bandara K, Hyndman RJ, Bergmeir C. MSTL. 2021; statsmodels.tsa.seasonal.STL/MSTL docs; Hua et al. Sci Adv 2025 (cosinor features).

### HMM and hidden semi-Markov models for recovery-state inference are inappropriate per-patient and only marginally defensible pooled
*[moderate]*

A 3-4 state HMM over recovery phases (acute perturbation / inflammatory resolution / functional recovery / complication) is conceptually attractive and matches how clinicians talk. The arithmetic does not support fitting it per patient: a Gaussian HMM with K states and d-dimensional emissions has K^2 transition parameters plus K*d means plus K*d*(d+1)/2 covariance parameters; at K=4, d=6 that is 16 + 24 + 84 = 124 free parameters against 90 daily observations. Baum-Welch will converge to a degenerate solution. Two viable options: (1) POOLED training with tied parameters — hmmlearn.hmm.GaussianHMM(n_components=3, covariance_type='diag', n_iter=100, tol=1e-3, init_params='mc', params='mct') fit on the concatenated sequences of all patients using the `lengths` argument, with covariance_type='diag' (not 'full') to cut parameters from 84 to 24, requires roughly 100+ patients before it is honest; (2) skip the HMM entirely and use BOCPD's run-length posterior as the state variable, which needs no training at all. The HSMM extension (explicit duration distributions, e.g. negative-binomial dwell times) is genuinely the right model for recovery — phase durations are emphatically not geometric — but Python support is thin (pyhsmm is unmaintained for modern NumPy; hsmmlearn is minimal) and it adds parameters we cannot afford. VERDICT: do not ship an HMM at 10-1000 patients. Revisit at >=300 patients with completed recoveries.

> Yu SZ. Hidden semi-Markov models. Artificial Intelligence 2010;174:215-243; hmmlearn documentation (hmmlearn.readthedocs.io); Rabiner LR. A tutorial on HMMs. Proc IEEE 1989;77:257-286.

### Gaussian-process deviation modelling: the right formal object for our expected-recovery curve, at a cost we probably should not pay yet
*[moderate]*

The natural GP formulation for our problem is a hierarchical/multi-task GP: f_i(d) = g_procedure(d) + h_i(d), where g is a population mean recovery function and h_i is a per-patient deviation with a small-amplitude kernel. Kernel choice: Matern(nu=1.5) for the patient deviation (physiological trajectories are rough, not infinitely smooth — RBF is too smooth and will under-cover), lengthscale prior around 7-14 days, plus a WhiteKernel for observation noise. sklearn: GaussianProcessRegressor(kernel=ConstantKernel(1.0)*Matern(length_scale=10.0, length_scale_bounds=(3,40), nu=1.5) + WhiteKernel(noise_level=0.5), normalize_y=True, n_restarts_optimizer=5, alpha=1e-6). The payoff over our current logistic curve f(d) = floor + (1-floor)/(1+exp(-r(d-d50))) is a per-day PREDICTIVE VARIANCE, so 'ratio-to-expected' becomes a properly calibrated z-score that automatically widens where few patients have data (very early and very late in recovery). The costs: O(n^3) per patient (irrelevant at n=90), but the hierarchical/multi-task version needs GPflow or GPyTorch, both of which drag in TensorFlow or PyTorch and are flatly incompatible with a lean AWS Lambda deployment. PRAGMATIC MIDDLE PATH: keep the parametric logistic curve as the mean function, and estimate the residual SD as a function of postoperative day empirically from the cohort (a simple binned or LOESS-smoothed SD by day), which delivers 80% of the benefit — day-varying, calibrated residuals — with zero new heavy dependencies.

> Rasmussen CE, Williams CKI. Gaussian Processes for Machine Learning. MIT Press 2006 (ch. 4, Matern class); scikit-learn GaussianProcessRegressor and Matern kernel documentation; Cheng LF et al. Sparse multi-output GPs for medical time series. 2020.

### Residual-based detection is the correct unifying architecture, and it is the one thing our current engine already gets right
*[strong]*

Every strong result in this literature is residual-based: Natarajan z-scores against a per-person rolling mean/SD; NightSignal differences against a streaming per-person median; TemPredict normalizes per participant; Ghomrawi uses 3-day rolling-average differences. Our engine already does the harder version of this correctly by judging functional metrics as a ratio to a procedure-specific expected recovery curve rather than to the pre-op baseline — a distinction Ghomrawi could not make and explicitly listed as a limitation ('unable to capture recovery relative to pre-surgical baseline'). The architectural rule to formalize: NOTHING is ever monitored in raw units. Every metric passes through a model that supplies the expected mean and variance for that patient on that postoperative day (Kalman local level for vitals, expected-recovery curve for functional metrics), and only the standardized residual reaches the detectors. Then EWMA, CUSUM, BOCPD, MEWMA and conformal all become interchangeable detectors operating on a common i.i.d.-under-null residual stream, which lets us swap and A/B them without touching the data layer. This also fixes a live defect: our CUSUM_WINDOW=14 truncation exists solely to stop drift-latching, and it disappears once CUSUM runs on innovations from a model that already tracks legitimate drift.

- REPLACE the composite thresholds high>2.0 / elevated>1.2 in composite.py with per-stratum conformal p-values (alpha=0.05 elevated, alpha=0.01 high). Any dashboard or investor material quoting AUROC without AUPRC and alarm burden is misleading.

Now (0 patients, 0 labels): NightSignal, Kalman innovations, BOCPD at lambda=1/35. A sensor-only alerting story is weaker than a fusion story, and the literature says so unambiguously.

Combine it with PELT segmentation to cross-validate the two label-free event definitions against each other.

## Recommended stack

- **Primary vitals alerting — replace the current raw-vitals EWMA/CUSUM as the headline detector** — NightSignal state machine on overnight resting HR, reimplemented verbatim from Alavi et al. Signal = mean HR over 00:00-07:00 restricted to zero-step minutes. Baseline = streaming median of all prior nights' overnight RHR (not the pre-op mean — the streaming median is robust and self-updating). Yellow when overnight RHR >= baseline + 3 bpm on one night; Red when >= baseline + 4 bpm on two consecutive nights; reset to Green when below threshold. via `None required — pure pandas/numpy, ~60 lines. pandas>=2.0 (already a dependency; present in backend/.venv).`
  - params: night_window = (00:00, 07:00); require step_count == 0; yellow_delta = 3.0 bpm; red_delta = 4.0 bpm; red_consecutive_nights = 2; baseline = expanding median with min_periods = 7; require >= 4 valid night-hours to score a night. Post-op adaptation: suppress alerts for postoperative days 0-1 (matches existing SKIP_EARLY_DAYS = 2) and seed the streaming median with the pre-op baseline median so it is valid from day 2 instead of day 7.
  - needs: Zero labels and zero training data — it is fully unsupervised and per-patient. Needs 7 nights of overnight HR to self-calibrate, or 0 extra nights if seeded from our pre-op baseline. Works from the very first patient.
  - why: It is the best-validated wearable alerting algorithm published — 80% sensitivity, 87.7% specificity, 3-day median lead on 84 cases — and in the authors' own head-to-head it beat CuSum (72%) and the LOF-based RHRAD (69%) at matched false-positive rate. It is also cheaper to compute than what we run today, has two interpretable parameters instead of four, and gives us a literature-anchored performance claim we can put in front of a clinician. Our pre-op baseline lets us start it on day 2 rather than day 7, which is strictly better than the published version.
- **Calibrated alerting threshold — replace the arbitrary composite thresholds high>2.0 / elevated>1.2 with a stated false-alarm rate** — Mondrian (per-procedure, per-postoperative-week) split conformal anomaly detection. Nonconformity score = the existing composite deviation index. Conformal p-value p_new = (#{i in calib : A_i >= A_new} + 1) / (n_calib + 1). Wrap in a power martingale for trajectory-level control. via `None required — ~25 lines of numpy. Optional: crepes>=0.7.0 or mapie>=0.8.0 if you want an audited implementation, both are pure-Python and Lambda-safe.`
  - params: Calibration set = all patient-days from patients who completed recovery with no adverse event, stratified by (procedure_type, postop_week_bucket). Alert at p <= 0.05 for 'elevated' and p <= 0.01 for 'high'. HARD CONSTRAINT: minimum attainable p is 1/(n_calib+1), so you need n_calib >= 99 days in a stratum to claim alpha=0.01 and n_calib >= 19 for alpha=0.05 — enforce this in code and degrade the label to 'insufficient calibration' otherwise. Sequential version: power martingale with epsilon = 0.92, alarm when M_t > 100 (Ville's inequality bounds trajectory false-alarm probability at 1/100).
  - needs: About 20 completed uneventful recoveries per procedure type (~20 x 90 = 1,800 patient-days, ample) to support alpha=0.05 within week buckets. Needs ZERO adverse-outcome labels — that is the point. Achievable inside the first 50 patients.
  - why: The current 2.0/1.2 cutoffs are unfalsifiable — they correspond to no known false-alarm rate, so we cannot tell a clinic how many nuisance alerts to expect. Conformal p-values convert the same composite score into a guaranteed-valid probability under exchangeability alone, with no labels and no distributional assumption. Given that Ghomrawi et al. logged 387-438 false positives across 162 patients, alarm burden is the metric that will decide whether clinicians keep using the product, and this is the only method here that lets us set it explicitly.
- **Personal baseline that legitimately drifts during recovery — replace fixed baseline + the CUSUM 14-day-window hack** — Kalman filter on a local level state-space model per patient per vital; feed the standardized one-step-ahead innovations v_t / sqrt(F_t) into the existing EWMA and CUSUM detectors instead of raw z-scores. via `statsmodels>=0.14.0 (statsmodels.tsa.statespace.structural.UnobservedComponents). Depends only on numpy/scipy/pandas which we already ship. ~15 MB in the Lambda bundle.`
  - params: UnobservedComponents(y, level='local level'). For n < 60 days do NOT run MLE — fix the signal-to-noise ratio q = sigma_eta^2/sigma_eps^2 = 0.02 (slow, credible physiological drift; equivalent steady-state EWMA lambda = K = (sqrt(q^2+4q)-q)/2 ≈ 0.13). Initialize the level at the pre-op baseline mean with initial variance = (pre-op SD)^2. Once >= 60 days and >= 40 non-missing observations, switch to .fit(method='lbfgs', maxiter=200) MLE with q bounded to [0.001, 0.10]. Then run the existing CUSUM (k=0.5, h=5.0) on the standardized innovations and DELETE CUSUM_WINDOW=14.
  - needs: Works per-patient from day one with the fixed-q setting; needs only the existing 14-21 day pre-op baseline for initialization. MLE estimation of q needs ~60 observations per patient, so it activates late in recovery or not at all. No labels, no cohort.
  - why: Our current EWMA is algebraically the steady-state special case of this filter, so this is a strict generalization, not a rewrite. It buys three things we need: principled handling of missing days (the Kalman filter marginalizes gaps natively, whereas pandas EWM silently mis-weights them, and our own confidence gate says gaps are common); a time-varying prediction variance F_t so control limits widen honestly when coverage is poor instead of us bolting on a separate confidence heuristic; and innovations that are genuinely i.i.d. N(0,1) under the null, which is the assumption CUSUM's h=5.0 threshold already depends on and currently violates.
- **Regime/phase change detection — a genuinely new capability ("this patient's recovery changed character on day 34")** — Bayesian Online Change Point Detection (Adams & MacKay) with constant hazard and a Normal-Inverse-Gamma conjugate prior, run on the standardized innovation stream from the Kalman stage. via `Implement directly — ~80 lines of numpy, no dependency, and this is preferable to the unmaintained `bayesian_changepoint_detection` package. Alternative if you want a package: `bocd` (pure numpy, MIT).`
  - params: Constant hazard H(r) = lambda = 1/35 (expected 35 days between genuine regime changes over a 90-day orthopedic recovery). DO NOT use the paper's 1/250 — it cannot fire within a single episode. Normal-Inverse-Gamma prior on the innovation stream: mu0 = 0, kappa0 = 1, alpha0 = 2.0 (finite variance, weakly informative), beta0 = 1.0 (since innovations are already standardized to unit scale). Prune run-length hypotheses with posterior mass < 1e-4 to keep O(1) per step. Emit a reason code when P(r_t < 3) > 0.5, i.e. the model believes a changepoint occurred within the last 3 days. Sensitivity-check lambda over {1/20, 1/35, 1/60} on retrospective data before pinning.
  - needs: Zero training data and zero labels — fully Bayesian and per-patient. Works from the first patient. Only needs the hazard rate to be set from clinical judgement about how often recovery regimes genuinely change.
  - why: BOCPD answers a question none of our current detectors can: not 'is today abnormal' but 'has this patient's physiological regime shifted, and how confident are we'. The run-length posterior is also a free, training-free substitute for the recovery-state HMM we cannot afford to fit, and it degrades gracefully — with no changepoint it simply reports a long run length. It is fully online (never revises past outputs, unlike PELT), closed-form, needs no optimizer, and is O(1) per day with pruning, so it is trivially Lambda-compatible.
- **Principled multivariate combination — replace the fixed-weight composite (RHR .25, temp .25, HRV .20, RR .15, steps .15)** — MEWMA on the vector of standardized vital innovations, with a shrunk covariance matrix pooled across patients within procedure type. via `numpy for the recursion (~30 lines); scikit-learn>=1.3 for sklearn.covariance.LedoitWolf. scikit-learn adds ~30 MB to the Lambda bundle — acceptable, and it is also needed for LocalOutlierFactor and BalancedRandomForest later.`
  - params: lambda = 0.15 (between NIST's 0.10 example and the 0.2 that is standard for faster response); statistic T2_i = Z_i' Sigma_Z^{-1} Z_i with Sigma_Z = [lambda/(2-lambda)] * [1-(1-lambda)^(2i)] * Sigma using the EXACT time-varying form, not the asymptotic one, because our series are only ~90 points and the asymptotic limit is not reached until i ≈ 30. Control limit h4 = 15.26 for p=6, lambda=0.1 at in-control ARL=200; use h4 = 16.0 for p=6, lambda=0.15 (interpolated — verify by simulation before shipping). COVARIANCE: Sigma must be a pooled correlation matrix estimated across ALL patients of the same procedure via LedoitWolf(assume_centered=True).fit(X).covariance_, then rescaled by each patient's own per-metric SDs. Never fit a per-patient 6x6 sample covariance from 14-21 baseline days — it is rank-deficient.
  - needs: Pooled Sigma needs roughly 10 x p = 60+ patient-days per procedure type at minimum, comfortably 200+ for stability — i.e. about 5-10 completed patients per procedure. Needs zero outcome labels. Do NOT enable this feature until the pooled covariance has >= 200 contributing patient-days; fall back to the fixed-weight composite below that.
  - why: Our current weights are asserted, not derived, and they ignore the fact that RHR, HRV and respiratory rate are strongly correlated — so the composite triple-counts a single autonomic disturbance and under-weights a genuinely independent signal like skin temperature. MEWMA handles the correlation structure explicitly and is provably more sensitive than Hotelling T-squared for the small sustained shifts (< 1.5 sigma) that characterize early infection. Keep the fixed-weight composite in parallel initially as the human-explainable version, and use MEWMA's disagreement with it as a QA signal.
- **Retrospective cohort analysis and label generation for the analytics team (offline, never patient-facing)** — PELT exact changepoint segmentation over completed recoveries, used to discover where recovery trajectories actually break, and to generate the pseudo-labels we lack. via `ruptures>=1.1.9 (numpy-only, small, Lambda-safe but should run offline in batch anyway).`
  - params: rpt.Pelt(model='rbf', min_size=7, jump=1).fit(signal).predict(pen=pen) for distributional change; model='l2' with pen = 2 * sigma_hat^2 * log(n) for pure mean shifts, where sigma_hat is the MAD-based residual SD. min_size=7 because a segment shorter than a week is not a clinical regime; jump=1 because n≈90 makes the speedup irrelevant. For the multivariate case use model='l2' on the stacked standardized residual matrix with pen = p * sigma_hat^2 * log(n), p = number of metrics. Sweep pen and pick via the elbow of the penalty-vs-nchangepoints curve.
  - needs: About 20-30 completed recoveries per procedure to be worth running. Zero labels. This is the tool that bootstraps everything else, so prioritize it early.
  - why: PELT is exact and offline, so it can revise its answer as data arrives — disqualifying for live alerting but ideal for the retrospective work we badly need: characterizing what a normal recovery trajectory looks like per procedure, calibrating the BOCPD hazard rate empirically, and defining in-control day sets for the conformal calibration corpus. It also gives us a defensible, unsupervised way to define 'event days' before we have any real outcome labels.
- **Supervised complication prediction — ONLY once outcome labels exist, and not before** — Balanced Random Forest with leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, exactly mirroring Ghomrawi 2023 and Hua 2025 (both independently selected BRF over XGBoost, AdaBoost, RUSBoost, balanced bagging and easy ensemble on this precise problem). via `imbalanced-learn>=0.12.0 (imblearn.ensemble.BalancedRandomForestClassifier), which requires scikit-learn>=1.3.`
  - params: BalancedRandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=500, sampling_strategy='all', replacement=True, bootstrap=False, max_depth=6, min_samples_leaf=5, class_weight=None, random_state=42). Validation: sklearn.model_selection.LeaveOneGroupOut with groups = patient_id — NEVER a random split, because patient-days within a patient are massively correlated and a random split will inflate AUC by 0.1+. Features: our existing standardized deviations, plus Ghomrawi's 3-day rolling-average differences, plus postoperative day, plus cosinor mesor/amplitude/acrophase once intraday data exists.

Realistically we need 50-100 labelled adverse events before a supervised model beats the unsupervised NightSignal+conformal stack, which at single-digit-percent prevalence means 1,000-2,000 monitored patients. Until then this row is a roadmap item, not a build item.
  - why: Two independent post-surgical wearable studies converged on this exact estimator for this exact problem, giving us a defensible prior rather than a search. BRF's internal per-tree undersampling of the majority class is the right inductive bias for single-digit-percent event rates, and it is interpretable enough (feature importances, and the model is a tree ensemble not a black box) to survive clinical review.

  - params: Cosinor: fit y(t) = M + A*cos(2*pi*t/24 + phi) as OLS; extract mesor M, amplitude A, acrophase phi, and model R^2 (Hua used model error as a feature in its own right — a poorly-fitting cosinor IS the anomaly). Loss of circadian amplitude is a well-established early sign of systemic inflammation, and it is completely invisible to daily-aggregate monitoring like ours. This is the clearest single upgrade path once data resolution improves.

## Open questions

- Does the NightSignal +3/+4 bpm threshold transfer to a post-operative population at all? Every reported validation is in ambulatory, non-surgical adults. Post-op patients have elevated resting HR from pain, opioids, beta-blocker interruption, anemia and deconditioning — the baseline is moving for entirely benign reasons for weeks. The thresholds may need to be expressed in patient-specific SD units rather than absolute bpm, or the streaming median may need to be replaced by the Kalman level so that legitimate post-op drift is absorbed. This must be checked empirically before the absolute-bpm rule is trusted; it is the single biggest transfer risk in this entire recommendation.
- What is the actual per-patient-day false-alarm rate of our CURRENT engine? We cannot claim any upgrade is an improvement without this baseline number, and I could not compute it from the code alone. This should be measured on whatever retrospective data exists before any of the proposed changes land.
- Is the exchangeability assumption underpinning conformal p-values defensible across postoperative days even after residualizing against the expected recovery curve? If residual variance is systematically larger in week 1 than week 8, marginal conformal validity holds but conditional validity fails, and alerts will cluster in early recovery. The proposed per-postop-week Mondrian stratification is the mitigation, but the bucket boundaries need to be chosen from data, not guessed.
- What is the true rate of genuine physiological regime changes per orthopedic recovery? The recommended BOCPD hazard of lambda=1/35 is a clinical guess, not an estimate. PELT run over completed recoveries should produce an empirical distribution of inter-changepoint intervals, and the hazard should be re-derived from it. Until then, treat lambda as a tuned hyperparameter and report sensitivity over {1/20, 1/35, 1/60}.
- Do we actually get intraday data, at what resolution, and from which devices? The largest single upgrade available — circadian/cosinor features, worth AUC 0.86 vs 0.80 in the two comparable post-surgical studies — is entirely gated on this. Also unresolved: whether wrist skin temperature from our devices is calibrated well enough to replicate TemPredict's 4.9% AUC gain, since Oura's finger-based dermal temperature is a substantially better sensor site than the wrist.
- How do we handle device heterogeneity? Ghomrawi flagged that a mid-study switch from Fitbit Inspire HR to Inspire 2 changed performance and that proprietary algorithm consistency could not be verified. If our patients bring their own devices (Apple Watch, Fitbit, Garmin, Oura), the per-patient baseline absorbs static offsets but NOT differences in how each vendor computes resting HR, HRV or sleep staging. We likely need device type as an explicit stratification variable in the conformal calibration, which multiplies the calibration data requirement.
- What is the clinically acceptable alarm burden, and who set it? This is a product and clinical-workflow question that determines the alpha we target, and no amount of methods work substitutes for asking the nurses who will triage the alerts. NightSignal's 1.15 alert-days per person for benign stress is a starting reference point, but the tolerable rate for a post-op orthopedic clinic is unknown to us.
- Should the composite index survive at all once MEWMA is available, or should it be retained purely as the clinician-facing explanation layer while MEWMA drives the tier? There is a real tension between the auditability that the current reason-code architecture provides and the statistical correctness of a covariance-aware statistic. A hybrid — MEWMA decides, composite explains — risks the two disagreeing in front of a clinician, which is worse than either alone.
- Do the post-operative baselines for HRV RMSSD and SpO2 have enough day-to-day stability to support any control chart? RMSSD in particular is notoriously noisy night to night, and the existing SD floor may be masking the fact that its effective signal-to-noise ratio does not support 2.66-sigma limits. Worth checking per-metric whether the SD floor is binding, and considering dropping metrics that never contribute a reason code.
- Is a hierarchical/multi-task Gaussian Process worth the deployment cost later, given that the pragmatic middle path (parametric logistic mean + empirically binned day-varying residual SD) captures most of the calibration benefit with zero new dependencies? Revisit only if the empirical residual-SD-by-day curve turns out to be too noisy to estimate from the cohort.
